Certification and standards

Nobody owns the word yet. Your record is the standard.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerNo universal regenerative certification exists yet, which is both a gap and an opportunity: the term is not owned, and organic certification alone does not require regeneration. What proves a regenerative farm is its own record: soil tests over seasons, input costs falling, and practice you can show. Sell on that, and never greenwash a claim the farm cannot demonstrate.

The certification landscape, honestly

The word regenerative is young and nobody owns it. Wikifarmer's 2025 agrifood trends report notes that regenerative agriculture is "yet to be universally defined and standardized," which means no certification body has won the term the way organic certifiers own theirs. What exists, by category:

  • Organic certification. Established, legally defined in most countries, and it verifies what a farm stops using: synthetic fertilizers and pesticides. It does not require regeneration: an organic-certified farm can till heavily, leave soil bare between crops, and lose topsoil every season. If you already farm organically by certificate, you have a legal floor, not a regenerative claim.
  • Private regenerative labels. Several organizations offer regenerative verification, each with its own standard. They are legitimate within their programs, but they are private: each defines the term its own way, none is universal, and enrollment costs. Before joining one, read what its standard actually requires and whether your farm already meets it.
  • Corporate supply-chain programs. Companies with agricultural supply chains run their own regenerative programs for their growers. Real, but crop-specific and relationship-driven: this reaches farms already organized into those supply chains.
  • No certification at all. The current reality for most regenerative farms, and worth stating plainly: the practice does not require a badge. What it requires is the record.
A certification describes a farm that already regenerates. It does not make one.

What actually proves a regenerative farm

Since no universal badge exists, the proof is the farm's own record, and the record is stronger than a label because it is specific, dated, and yours. What a buyer, a cooperative, or a neighbor can verify:

  • The soil record. Worm counts, infiltration times, and crumb structure, written down season by season in the same fields. A trend line, not a claim. This is the core of the regenerative case and it is what the measurement module builds.
  • The input record. What the farm stopped buying: fertilizer purchases falling, the tiller idling, the spray calendar empty. Receipts and a notebook beat any attestation.
  • The practice, visible. Cover on the soil, residue mulching the beds, diverse crops in the ground, habitat on the boundaries. A farm running the system shows it to anyone who walks it.
  • The story, told honestly. Why the farm changed, what it cost, what it returned. The story is the part buyers remember, and it is only credible when it includes the hard parts.

That record is also what any future certification will ask for, which means the farm that keeps it is ready for whatever standard arrives without changing how it farms.

How to avoid greenwashing your own claims

Greenwashing is usually accidental: a farm or a brand says something its practice cannot demonstrate. The rules that keep claims honest, and they apply to this site as much as to any farm:

  • Claim only what the record shows. "Soil organic matter improving, verified by worm counts over three seasons" is a claim. "Our soil is healthy" is a vibe. The first survives scrutiny; the second invites it.
  • Never claim a result you have not measured. Yields, carbon numbers, water savings: if the farm has not measured it, it is not a claim, it is a hope, and hopes do not belong in a sales conversation.
  • Say the hard parts. The first season was flat, the transition cost labor, some pests still arrive. A claim with the hard parts included is credible; a claim without them is marketing, and buyers discount it.
  • Do not borrow other farms' numbers. Another farm's yield or certification is their evidence, not yours. Cite it as theirs or leave it out.
  • Do not use the badge as the claim. A certification, when you eventually join one, describes the practice. The practice is the claim; the badge is its shorthand.

Selling on verified practice

The commercial upside of a real record: a farm that can show its soil trend, its falling input costs, and its practice sells to buyers who pay for verified growing, at prices the commodity market does not offer. The path:

  • Direct to buyers. Restaurants, cooperatives, and households that pay for how their food is grown. The record is what makes the conversation work: the farm's trend line is the product's story.
  • Through a cooperative. Pooled volume with verified practice reaches buyers an individual farm cannot, and the cooperative carries the marketing that the farm's record feeds.
  • At market, in person. The cheapest version: the farm's own stall, the notebook on the table, the story told by the farmer who farmed it. Small volume, full margin, and the credibility compounds.

This is module nine's territory in detail; what matters here is that the record and the selling are the same system: the farm that measures can prove, and the farm that can prove can sell on it.

When to join a program, and when not to

The decision rule that works: join a verification program when its standard describes what the farm already does and its cost is worth the market it opens. Do not join one to become regenerative, because the practice comes first and the program verifies it. And never let a program's requirements push the farm into practices its own ground does not suit: a standard written for temperate row crops will not fit a tropical smallholding, and the farm that bends to fit a label has traded its system for someone else's.

The Farmers Association and the cooperative are the practical middle path: they verify within the community, they know the local ground, and they carry the farm's record to buyers without the enrollment cost of a private program.

Systematizing what you already do

The steward reading this page already farms regeneratively, mostly by instinct. The work of this stage is turning instinct into a system: the practices named, the tests written down, the calendar explicit. Three reasons it is worth doing. First, instinct does not transfer: the next generation, or a hired hand, or a neighbor learning from you needs the system, not the instincts. Second, instinct cannot prove anything: the record is what sells the farm's practice. Third, instinct has gaps: the practices you do by habit are the ones you never questioned, and writing them down is how the gaps show up. The measurement module and the course are the systematizing tools.

The standards your buyers actually ask about

Certification is one way to verify practice, and the buyers who pay a premium ask about others. What actually comes up in the conversation, by category:

  • The record. The notebook: soil tests over seasons, input costs falling, harvests. This is what a serious buyer asks for first, because it cannot be faked cheaply and it shows the direction the farm is going.
  • A walk of the farm. Cover on the soil, residue mulching the beds, diversity in the ground, habitat on the boundaries. A buyer who walks the ground sees the practice in ten minutes, and most of the premium conversation happens there rather than in paperwork.
  • References and story. The cooperative, the association, the neighbors who have watched the transition. The story told by the farmer who farmed it, hard parts included, is what the buyer remembers.
  • Any certificate the farm holds. Organic or a private regenerative label, where it exists. It verifies a floor, not the whole practice, and it sits in the conversation as one document among several.

The pattern across all four: the record and the practice carry the verification, and the paperwork supports it. A farm that has the first two can sell to premium buyers today; the certificate, when it eventually arrives, adds a document to a conversation that was already working.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a certification for regenerative farming?

No universal one exists yet. The term is not owned by any certification body, the way organic certifiers own theirs. Private regenerative labels exist with their own standards, and corporate supply-chain programs verify their own growers. What proves a regenerative farm today is its own record, which is also what any future certification will ask for.

Does organic certification mean a farm is regenerative?

No. Organic certifies what a farm stops using, synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, and does not require regeneration. An organic farm can till heavily, leave soil bare, and lose topsoil every season. Organic is a legal floor; regenerative is a direction of improvement.

How do I prove my farm is regenerative?

With its own record: soil tests written down season by season, input costs that fell, practice that is visible to anyone who walks the farm, and the story told honestly, hard parts included. The record is stronger than a badge because it is specific, dated, and yours.

What is greenwashing, and how do I avoid it?

Claiming something your practice cannot demonstrate, usually by accident. The rules: claim only what the record shows, never claim unmeasured results, say the hard parts, do not borrow other farms' numbers, and treat any certification as shorthand for the practice rather than as the claim itself.
