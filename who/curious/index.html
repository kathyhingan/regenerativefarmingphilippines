Why regenerative farming matters

The honest benefits, and the honest costs. Both, before you decide.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerRegenerative farming's real benefits: input costs fall first, soil holds water through dry weeks, the farm rides out typhoons better, and income stops depending on one crop. The honest costs: a learning curve, a first season with flat results, timing discipline, and neighbors who will question a mulched field. The benefits compound; the costs are front-loaded.

The benefits of regenerative agriculture, honestly ranked

Most articles list the benefits in the order that markets well. Here they are in the order a farmer actually feels them:

  1. Input costs fall, first and fastest. Compost from straw and manure replaces purchased fertilizer. A cover crop replaces herbicide. Mulch replaces watering hours. This is the benefit that arrives in the first season, and it is the one that makes every other change possible: the farm saves money while it learns.
  2. The soil starts holding water. Covered, biologically active ground absorbs rain instead of shedding it, and organic matter holds moisture like a sponge. In the dry weeks this is the difference between irrigating daily and irrigating weekly. In the wet season it is the difference between soil that drinks and soil that washes away.
  3. The farm gets resilient. Diverse crops mean one failed crop or one crashed price is not the year. Deep roots and covered soil mean a typhoon takes less with it. This benefit is invisible in good years and the whole story in bad ones.
  4. The work changes character. The hours stop being a treadmill of repeated chores (tilling, spraying, buying) and become the season's actual work. On a family farm this is not a small thing: it is the difference between the farm running the family and the family running the farm.
  5. Health, for what it is worth here. Fewer synthetic sprays on the farm means fewer for the family and the neighbors to breathe, touch, and drink from the water table. This is the benefit that markets well, and it is real, but it is listed fifth because the farmer deciding to change is deciding on economics first.
The benefits compound. The costs are front-loaded. That asymmetry is the whole decision.

The honest disadvantages

Every method has costs, and the regenerative transition's are real. Stated plainly, because a farmer deciding deserves them:

  • A learning curve. Reading soil, timing compost, managing cover crops: these are skills, and skills take seasons. The first year you will get things wrong that a textbook makes look easy, and some of the imported advice will not translate to your climate at all.
  • A first season with flat results. The harvest does not jump in year one. The input costs fall, but the yields hold or dip while the biology establishes. A farmer expecting a first-season transformation quits; a farmer expecting a first-season direction stays.
  • Timing discipline. Cutting cover crops before they seed, applying compost before the rains, watering in the morning: the method rewards attention to the calendar, and it penalizes the farm that plants by habit.
  • The neighbors. A mulched field looks abandoned to a barangay of bare-tilled farms. The comments come before the results, and the second season's field is the only answer that works.
  • Some pests still arrive. The system prevents most outbreaks, not all, and the transition seasons may lose more while the habitat establishes. The response is scouting and farm-made sprays rather than the calendar, which is a skill of its own.

What changes first, season by season

The transition is easier to decide on when the order of change is spelled out, because the benefits do not arrive together:

  • Season one: the input costs fall. Compost replaces a portion of the fertilizer, the cover crop replaces a spray, the mulch replaces watering hours. The yields hold or dip. The soil tests start moving. This is the season that tests patience, and the notebook is what makes it survivable.
  • Season two: the worms and the water infiltration improve visibly. Weed pressure drops where the mulch went down. The workload shifts from chores to the season's work. The budget gap between the old way and the new one opens.
  • Season three: the topsoil darkens, the beds hold structure between crops, and the yields stabilize above the old average in good years. The farm now rides out a dry spell or a typhoon that would have cost the old system a season.

That sequence is why the honest answer to "is it worth it" is: the economics turn in year one, the soil answers in year two, and the resilience shows in year three. A farmer who knows the order can decide; a farmer promised everything at once is being sold to.

What the change looked like on our farm

The transition ran on our own ground the way it will run on yours: the first season uncomfortable, the neighbors' comments arriving before the results, the compost pile going wrong once before it went right. What made the difference was smaller than any technique: the straw stopped being burned, the mulch went on, and the notebook started holding what the farm was actually doing.

By the second season the field answered: worms back, water soaking in where it used to run off, the hoe coming out half as often. By the third, the beds held their structure on their own and the input bill had fallen enough that the arithmetic was no longer an argument. That is the transition, honestly: a first season of direction, a second of evidence, a third of compound interest.

The question behind the question

Most farmers asking whether regenerative farming matters are really asking two different questions, and it is worth separating them. The first is "will this make my farm money," and the honest answer is that the input costs fall first and the rest compounds over seasons. The second is "is this worth it for reasons beyond money," and that answer depends on what you want the farm to be when your children inherit it. On our farm the second question was the one that mattered: the land had been in the family for three generations, the alternative was watching it become another industrial lot, and the economics turned out to work anyway.

Both questions have honest answers. A farmer who needs only the first can stop at the arithmetic. A farmer who needs both is the reason this family teaches at all.

What you keep, and what you stop losing

The benefits are usually listed as gains, but half of them are losses that stop. The topsoil that washed away every wet season stays. The straw that burned as smoke becomes the mulch that holds next season's moisture. The fertilizer bill that climbed every year falls as the compost pile takes over its job. The hours that went to repeated chores become the season's actual work. A farm running this system is not adding new things so much as it is closing the leaks the old system had, and the leaks were the most expensive part of it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest benefit of regenerative farming?

Input costs falling, first and fastest. Compost from straw and manure replaces purchased fertilizer, cover crops replace herbicide, and mulch replaces watering hours. It is the benefit that arrives in the first season, and it is what makes every other change possible.

What are the disadvantages of regenerative farming?

A learning curve that takes seasons, a first season with flat or dipping yields while the biology establishes, timing discipline that rewards attention to the calendar, neighbors who question a mulched field, and some pest pressure during the transition. The costs are front-loaded; the benefits compound.

Is regenerative farming worth it for a small farm?

Yes, and small farms have the advantage: the transition is cheaper to start, one person can run it, and the results show faster at small scale. The first season is built on what the farm already produces, which is a packet of seed and the straw that was being burned anyway.

How do I know if regenerative farming is right for my farm?

Read the definition and the arithmetic, take the Find Your Path quiz to see which module your farm should start with, and try the first season on one field with what the farm already produces. The notebook will tell you whether the direction is real before you commit the whole farm.
