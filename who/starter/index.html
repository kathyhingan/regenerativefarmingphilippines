Regenerative farming for beginners

You have decided. Here is the first season, sized honestly.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerBegin regenerative farming by starting smaller than you think: cover the bare soil, start one compost pile, and write down what happens. The first season runs on what the farm already produces, a packet of seed and the straw that was being burned. Skip the big purchases and the whole-farm conversion; one bed done properly converts the farmer.

Start smaller than you think

The beginner's instinct is to convert everything at once, and it is the reason most regenerative attempts fail. One field, one bed, or one backyard plot, done properly, teaches more than a whole farm attempted. It is where you learn to read the soil, time the compost, and manage the cover without the workload shock of doing it everywhere, and it is where the results show fastest because you can watch every square meter of it.

The size that works: whatever piece of ground you can cover, watch, and water without help. A backyard bed, a corner of a field, a paddies' edge. The method scales up later; the learning does not scale down from a whole-farm attempt.

The first three things to do

  1. Cover every patch of bare soil. Rice straw, crop residue, or a fast cover crop like munggo. This is the cheapest, highest-impact action on the farm, it starts the day you do it, and it is the foundation everything else sits on. Bare soil is losing moisture, biology, and topsoil every day it is exposed; the cover stops all three.
  2. Start one compost pile. A cubic meter, from straw layered with manure, watered, turned when the center cools. Everything the recipe needs is already on the farm, and the pile is running before you need its output. The first pile teaches the balance; the second is easy.
  3. Write down what happens. The four soil tests (worm count, infiltration, smell, crumb) one line each with the date, plus what you spend and what you harvest. This is the step beginners skip and the one that decides whether they stay: the notebook is what turns a flat first season into a direction you can see.

Those three things are the whole first season, sized honestly. Everything else in the method builds on them, and none of it works without them.

What to skip in year one

  • Equipment. No tiller attachments, no brewing hardware, no tools beyond what the farm has. Every first-season practice runs on what the farm already produces. The farm that buys its way in learns nothing and spends what the transition was meant to save.
  • The whole-farm conversion. Stopping tillage everywhere at once produces a weed flush and a workload shock. One field converts the farmer; the farmer converts the farm.
  • Five crops at once. The diversity the method builds is real, but it is built over seasons. Two or three crops plus a cover, done properly, is a first season. Nine things attempted is none of them established.
  • The premium market plan. A consistent harvest comes before a premium buyer, and consistency takes seasons. Planning the market in year one is promising what the system cannot yet fill.
  • Imported advice, unadapted. Most regenerative farming content is temperate. It assumes spring and fall, a winter dormancy, and a calendar built for four seasons. Use the principles, adapt the timing, and be suspicious of any instruction that does not make sense in a wet and dry cycle.

Backyard and smallholder scale

The method proves itself fastest at small scale, and it is worth saying why: a backyard bed is small enough to watch every day, cheap enough to experiment on, and fast enough to show results within a season. The worm count improves in weeks on a bed that is composted, mulched, and left undug. The structure changes visibly. The weeds drop. A season of that converts a farmer more effectively than any book, which is why the recommendation for beginners with no land is to start in containers or a friend's backyard rather than waiting for a farm.

At smallholder scale the same logic applies to the whole holding: the compost pile, the cover on the gaps, and the notebook are all cheaper per square meter than on a large farm, and the family's labor is the farm's labor. The scale that matters is not the area; it is whether the system is running.

The calendar, sized for your first year

  • Now: cover the bare soil. Whatever the season, this is first.
  • Before the wet season: compost applied and mulched, covers established, drainage checked. The rains then work for the farm instead of against it.
  • The wet season: watch what the covered ground does that the bare ground does not. This is the most convincing season on the calendar.
  • The dry season: mulch is the irrigation. The beds that went in covered hold their moisture; the ones that did not tell you why the cover matters.
  • Season's end: read the notebook honestly. Mostly flat numbers are normal; the direction is the result.

The beginner mistakes that cost a year

Learned the expensive way, on our own ground, so you can skip them:

  • Tilling the mulch in. The straw layer is working on the surface; burying it restarts the damage and wastes the season's best material. Feed from the top and let the worms take it down.
  • The compost pile that stopped. Left dry in the hot weeks or waterlogged in the rains, a pile goes cold and stays there. Walk it every few days: water it in the dry, cover it in the wet. The pile that went wrong taught the balance better than any recipe.
  • Cover crops gone to seed. One uncut strip becomes next season's weed problem. Mark the cutting date when you sow, and walk the strip weekly as flowering starts.
  • Spraying before identifying. The calendar-spray habit survives the transition longer than the tiller. Scout first: most damage is from a handful of culprits, each with a different answer.
  • Quitting on the first season's numbers. The first season is flat on purpose: the system is being built, not the harvest. The notebook holds the direction, and the direction is the result.

Why beginners have an advantage

Counterintuitive, and true: a beginner starting on one bed is in a better position than a farmer converting a running farm. Nothing to unlearn, nothing in the ground to risk, and the whole system small enough to watch daily. The worm count on a composted, mulched, undug bed moves in weeks, the structure changes visibly in a season, and every mistake is cheap. An experienced farmer converting a whole operation has more at stake in every decision and a longer feedback loop on all of them.

The practical use of that advantage: learn on the smallest piece of ground you control, make the mistakes there, and let the notebook hold the evidence. By the time the method scales up to the whole farm, the farmer is no longer a beginner on any of it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start regenerative farming as a beginner?

Three things: cover every patch of bare soil with straw or a fast cover crop, start one compost pile from materials the farm already produces, and write down the soil tests and costs with dates. That is the whole first season, and it runs on a packet of seed and the straw that was being burned anyway.

Can I practice regenerative farming in a backyard?

Yes, and it is the fastest place to learn: a backyard bed is small enough to watch daily, cheap enough to experiment on, and shows worm activity and better structure within a single season. Containers work too. The method scales up; the learning does not scale down from a whole-farm attempt.

What equipment does a beginner need?

Almost none. The first season runs on what the farm already has: a hoe or spade for opening beds, a container for the biofertilizer brew, a notebook, and the straw and manure the farm already produces. The big purchases come later, if ever, and only when the system has proven itself.

How long before I see results as a beginner?

At backyard or small-plot scale, within the first season: worms return to a composted, mulched, undug bed in weeks, and the structure changes visibly. At field scale the soil results take one to three seasons, and the input costs fall first, often in the first year.
