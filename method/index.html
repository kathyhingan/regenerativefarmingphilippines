The Method

Ten modules. One farm. Your land next.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerThe Method is ten modules that take a farm from depleted soil to a paying, resilient system: why to change, soil biology, compost from farm materials, no-till, cover and layers, pests without poisons, water and tropical seasons, a first-season plan, the economics, and how to measure progress.

What each module does

Every module answers one question and ends with one thing to do on your own land. They are listed here in the order a transition actually happens, because that order is the method: you cannot judge whether your compost is working before you can read soil, and you cannot sell a premium crop before the soil under it is holding water.

The ten modules in detail

1. Why Regenerative, Why Now

Rising input costs, degraded soil, and what the family legacy means. The honest case for changing, before any technique. This module also settles what the word actually means, so you are not chasing a label that no certifier owns yet.

Read the Why Regenerative, Why Now module summary

2. Know Your Soil

Living soil science in plain language, and four tests a farmer can run with no laboratory: a worm count, a jar test, a water infiltration test, and the smell and crumb of a handful of earth. You end this module able to say whether your soil is improving, in numbers you wrote down yourself.

Read the Know Your Soil module summary

3. Compost and Biofertilizers

Fertility from rice straw, manure, kakawate, madre de cacao, coconut coir, and crop residues. Brewing and applying, with the costs avoided spelled out. This is the module that pays for the course fastest, because it replaces a recurring purchase with material the farm was already burning or hauling away.

Read the Compost and Biofertilizers module summary

4. Stop Tilling

Minimal disturbance adapted to rice paddies and vegetable beds, and what happens to soil structure, fungal networks, and weeds when you stop. It also covers the honest exceptions: when a one-time pass is the right call, and how to avoid trading a tillage problem for a weed problem.

Read the Stop Tilling module summary

5. Cover the Soil

Cover crops and the seven-layer planting: canopy trees, vines, cash crops, covers, roots, groundcover, and animals in one system. You leave with a planting plan for your own plot, using species that grow here, and a termination schedule so the cover does not become the next weed.

Read the Cover the Soil module summary

6. Pests Without Poisons

Why a diverse, covered, biologically active system gets fewer pest problems, plus scouting, beneficial insects, and sprays made from farm materials. It is honest about the limits: some outbreaks still need a targeted intervention, and the module tells you which ones and when.

Read the Pests Without Poisons module summary

7. Water and Seasons

The tropical wet and dry calendar instead of a temperate one, typhoon resilience, and irrigation timing that matches how rain actually arrives. It also gives the honest carbon story, that soil carbon is real but slow, and that carbon credit schemes are a separate and complicated topic.

Read the Water and Seasons module summary

8. The First-Season Conversion Plan

A step-by-step first year on your own land: observe before changing anything, cover the bare soil, start one compost pile, stop tilling one field, and write down what happens. It says plainly what to skip in year one, which is most of what beginners try to do at once.

Read the The First-Season Conversion Plan module summary

9. Making It Pay

Where the money actually comes from, in order: reduced input costs first, then premium sales, then direct and cooperative marketing. Dragonfruit is the worked case, the vine layer that pays while the other layers build soil, and grants are covered honestly, including the fact that they are country-specific and never guaranteed.

Read the Making It Pay module summary

10. Measure and Keep Going

Soil health indicators year over year, the notebook that becomes your farm's record, cost tracking in your own currency, and the honest timelines for when soil changes show. It also covers what to do when results do not show, which is the point where most farmers quit.

Read the Measure and Keep Going module summary

How to work through them

Two honest routes, depending on where your farm is right now. If you are still using chemical inputs and unsure whether any of this is viable, start at module one, then go straight to module three, because compost is where a farmer feels the difference in the wallet first. If you have already decided and need the method, work the modules in order, one a week, doing the work each one asks for before moving on.

If you are already farming regeneratively and want to systematize, go to module nine and ten first, then loop back to the ones you have been doing by instinct. Reading them in order after years of practice usually shows why the instincts worked.

Why the order matters

Most failed transitions start in the wrong place. Farmers who buy amendments and brews before they have living roots and covered soil are feeding a biology that is not there yet. Farmers who stop tilling everything in one season, without a cover crop already growing, get a weed flush and conclude the method does not work. Farmers who chase a premium market before the soil can produce consistently end up with a promise they cannot fill.

The sequence removes those traps: understand the case, learn to read your soil, make your own fertility, reduce disturbance, keep something growing, handle pests and water, plan the first season properly, then work the economics and measurement.

The sequence is the method. Skip a step and the step you skipped comes back as a failed season.

What sits inside each module

Every module has the same four parts, so a farmer always knows what to expect:

  • The answer. A short lesson in text and video that answers the module's question directly, usually five to fifteen minutes.
  • The farm example. How this exact thing is done on the family's own land in Laguna, including what went wrong the first time.
  • The work. One task on your own land, sized to a single season, with the results written into a notebook rather than remembered.
  • The test. How to tell whether it worked, using the soil tests from module two or the cost numbers from module nine.

That structure is deliberate. Watching a lesson changes nothing on a farm. The module only counts when the work has been done and the test has an answer.

What the modules are not

They are not a certification and not a promise of yield. Soil changes take seasons, and some of the gains show up in cost and resilience before they show in volume. What the modules give you is the order to work in and the tests to know whether it is working, on your land, in your own seasons.

The farm the modules came from

Every module in this list was farmed before it was written. The family has worked this land in Calamba, Laguna for three generations: grandparents planting rice, sugarcane, vegetables, and calamansi, and the father continuing after his mother passed, now leading the barangay Farmers Association and working with the City of Calamba on farmer education. Today the farm's main crop is dragonfruit, with animals in the system and the seven-layer planting laid out across the property.

The land around the farm converted to industrial use over the decades. The family kept farming it. That continuity is the reason these modules read like field notes instead of theory, and it is the reason the tropical season timing in module seven exists at all: nobody here had to translate it from a book about spring and fall.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to do all ten modules in order?

Order is recommended, not enforced. The sequence exists because each module assumes the previous one's work is in place, so skipping ahead usually means redoing a step later. If you only have time for two modules this season, take soil and compost, which is where the fastest practical return sits.

Which module should a rice farmer start with?

Start with soil, then compost, then tilling. Rice straw and animal manure are the two cheapest fertility sources a rice farm already has, and the paddy is where reducing disturbance and keeping residue on the field changes both water holding and input cost most quickly.

Are the modules useful outside the Philippines?

The principles travel anywhere, and the practice details are tropical-first. Farmers in other tropical regions can apply the modules directly. Farmers in temperate climates can use the soil, compost, and economics modules as they are, and will need to translate the season calendar to their own growing year.

Where do I start if I only have a garden?

The same modules, at smaller scale, and they tend to show results faster. A garden bed with added compost, a mulch layer, and no digging will show worm activity and better structure within one season, which makes it a good place to learn the method before applying it to a field.
