Stop tilling

Every pass of the tiller undoes a season of good work.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerNo-till farming means disturbing the soil as little as possible: no routine rototilling, no plowing out of habit. Every pass breaks fungal networks and releases carbon, so the gains from compost and cover crops are undone each season. You stop by keeping the soil covered, opening beds by hand, and letting biology do the work tillage used to do.

What tillage actually does underground

Tillage looks harmless from above: a neat, fluffy seedbed, ready for planting. Underground it is a demolition. The rototiller's tines slice through the fungal networks that thread the soil, the same networks that carry water and nutrients to roots. It collapses the crumb structure that lets air and water move. It buries the surface biology in the dark and exposes the buried biology to the sun. And it releases a pulse of carbon to the air, because the sudden rush of oxygen makes the bacteria burn through organic matter that would have lasted years.

The fluffy seedbed itself is the tell. That fluffiness is the structure being broken, and it settles into compaction within a season, which is why tilled soil needs tilling again. The farm ends up on a treadmill: till, compact, till harder, compact more. Every pass feels like preparation and costs more than it prepares.

The fluffy seedbed is not healthy soil. It is the wreckage of healthy soil, and it settles.

What happens when you stop

The first season after stopping is the uncomfortable one, and it is honest to say so. Without tillage keeping the surface loose, crusted or compacted ground stays that way until biology and roots reopen it. Weeds that tillage used to bury arrive on the surface and have to be handled by mulch and competition instead. The transition usually looks like this:

  • First weeks: nothing looks better. The soil is as it was. This is the observe period, not the failure period.
  • First season: the surface may crust where it was already degraded, and weed pressure feels higher because you are seeing weeds tillage used to hide. A thick mulch layer handles both.
  • Second season: worms and roots have started reopening the topsoil. The infiltration test improves. Weed pressure drops as the mulch and cover crops outcompete germination.
  • Year three: the soil holds its structure on its own. Beds stay open between seasons. The need for any disturbance keeps falling, which is the point.

How to transition without losing the crop

Going cold turkey on degraded soil is the mistake that gives no-till a bad name. The transition that works on a tropical farm:

  1. Cover first. Before you stop tilling anything, get mulch or a cover crop onto the surface. The cover is what replaces tillage's weed control and moisture work. Stopping tillage on bare soil is how you get both a crust and a weed flush.
  2. Stop one field, not the whole farm. Pick the field or bed with the best soil, or the smallest one, and make it the test. The rest of the farm keeps running while you learn what the transition looks like on your own ground.
  3. Open beds by hand. A hoe or a spade, opening a planting hole or a strip, disturbs a fraction of what a tiller does. Hand work is not a romantic choice on a small farm; it is a precision one.
  4. Use the paddy's own cycle. Rice is the exception that helps: a paddy is flooded for half the year, which suppresses weeds biologically. Lowland rice farmers can reduce tillage between crops and let the flood plus a straw mulch do the work, which is a smaller change than in a dryland system.
  5. Let the residues lie. Previous crop's straw and trash stay on the surface as mulch. Direct seeding or transplanting through that residue is the no-till mechanic, and it gets easier every season as the residue layer builds.

The honest exceptions: when tilling is still the right call

No-till is a direction, not a religion, and three cases justify a pass:

  • A hardpan or plow layer. Compacted layer deep enough that roots and water cannot get through sometimes needs mechanical breaking, once. A deep-rooted cover crop like radish or pigeon pea can do the same job biologically, but it takes seasons. Breaking the pan once, then covering the ground and never doing it again, is the trade that works.
  • A brand-new bed in degraded ground. Opening a first bed in soil that has been dead for years is a legitimate single pass, paired immediately with compost and a cover so the bed starts regenerating the day it is opened.
  • A rescue. A soil that has gone anaerobic, a salt problem, a spot that needs regrading. Rare, real, and better handled with one honest pass than with a dogmatic rule.

The difference between these and routine tillage is that they are decisions, made once, with a regenerative plan attached. The treadmill is not a decision; it is a habit.

The first season without a tiller, on our farm

The transition looked like this on our own ground, and it was not smooth. The first season after the last routine pass, the beds crusted where the soil had been tilled for years, and the weeds that used to be buried arrived in force. The mulch layer, rice straw over every bed, was what held the line: it kept the crust from baking and shaded out most of the germination while the biology underneath restarted.

By the second season the difference was measurable rather than visible: the worm count in the beds went up, water stopped pooling the way it used to, and the hoe came out half as often. What had looked like doing nothing was the biology and the cover doing the work the tiller used to do, without the fuel or the damage. The third season, the beds held their structure on their own between crops, and preparation became a hand job rather than a machine job.

The honest lesson from that transition: the first season tests your patience, not the method. A farmer who judges no-till by its first months will quit; a farmer who judges it by its second year keeps the farm.

What replaces each tillage job

Most farmers keep tilling because it does four jobs, and each job has a replacement:

  • Weed control: replaced by mulch, cover crops, and competition. A thick straw layer suppresses germination better than a hoe, and it works every day instead of once a pass.
  • Moisture work: replaced by the mulch layer as well. Tilled bare soil loses moisture fast; covered soil keeps it.
  • Seedbed preparation: replaced by hand-opened holes and strips, and by soil that holds its structure between seasons so there is less to prepare.
  • Incorporating fertilizer: replaced by surface compost and mulch, which feed from the top down the way the biology expects. Tilling compost in buries the biology that needs it near the surface.

What the neighbors will say

Honest, because it stops more transitions than any agronomic problem: a fallow-looking field with mulch on it draws comment in a barangay where every farm is tilled bare. The neighbors will ask if the farm has been abandoned. Some will tell you the straw should be burned. The answer that works is the field itself, once the second season shows what the soil is doing, and until then the only thing that matters is your own notebook: the worm counts, the infiltration times, and the input costs that move while the farm looks untidy.

This is also why the barangay Farmers Association work matters. A farmer transitioning alone argues with everyone; a group of farmers comparing notes argues with results. The association is where those notes get compared, and it is the reason the method is taught publicly at all.

How it connects to the rest of the method

No-till is module four of ten, right after compost and biofertilizers and before cover crops. The order matters: a cover crop and a compost pile should already exist before the last tiller pass, because they are what replace it. Start at the definition if you are new, and see the first-season plan for the transition laid out as a year.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to stop tilling completely?

No. The goal is minimal disturbance, not zero. A one-time pass to break a hardpan or open a first bed in dead ground is a legitimate decision. What the method removes is routine tillage, the seasonal pass that exists because it always has.

Will weeds get worse when I stop tilling?

They get more visible in the first season, because tillage used to bury them, then they get better as mulch and cover crops suppress germination. The transition's weed flush is temporary; a farm with a thick permanent cover has less weed pressure than a tilled one within two seasons.

Can a rice paddy be farmed without tillage?

Partially, and the flood helps: a paddy is submerged for half the year, which suppresses weeds biologically. Between crops, keep the straw on the field as mulch and reduce the passes between cycles. Lowland rice is one of the easier systems to transition because the flood does part of the work.

What do I do about hard, compacted soil without tilling it?

Two routes: a deep-rooted cover crop like radish or pigeon pea to break the layer biologically over a couple of seasons, or one honest mechanical pass to break the pan, immediately followed by permanent cover. Both work; what matters is that it happens once, not every season.
