What is regenerative agriculture?

A working definition, from a family that farms.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerRegenerative agriculture is farming that rebuilds soil health instead of depleting it. It rests on five practices: living roots, covered soil, minimal disturbance, crop diversity, and feeding soil biology. It differs from organic by what it builds toward, not just what it avoids, and it is learned season by season.

The definition

Regenerative agriculture is a way of farming that rebuilds soil health instead of depleting it, by keeping living roots in the soil, keeping the soil covered, disturbing it as little as possible, growing a diversity of crops, and feeding the soil biology rather than only feeding the plant.

That is the working definition this whole site is built on. Notice what it does not say: it does not say regenerative agriculture is a product you buy, a certificate you earn, or a rulebook you follow. It is a direction of travel. A farm is regenerative to the degree that its soil, its biology, and its profitability are all improving year over year, and conventional to the degree that they are not.

This matters because the word is young. Wikifarmer's 2025 agrifood trends report notes that regenerative agriculture is "yet to be universally defined and standardized," which means no certification body owns the term and no single definition has won. That is not a weakness in the word. It is the reason a farmer can still decide for themselves what the standard should be, on their own land, and why we wrote ours down.

The five practices, in plain language

Every working definition of regenerative agriculture converges on a small set of practices. Ours has five, and each one is something a farmer can start this season:

  1. Keep living roots in the soil. Roots feed the biology underground. Bare soil between crops is hungry soil. A cover crop or a cash crop, something growing, all year.
  2. Keep the soil covered. Mulch, residue, a living cover. Covered soil holds water, resists weeds, and does not bake or wash away in the tropical sun and rain.
  3. Disturb the soil as little as possible. Every pass of a tiller breaks the fungal networks and opens the soil to erosion. Less disturbance, more structure.
  4. Grow a diversity of crops. Monoculture feeds one pest and one market. Diversity spreads both risks. On our farm that is the seven-layer planting: canopy trees, vines, cash crops, cover crops, roots, groundcover, and animals in the same system.
  5. Feed the soil biology, not just the plant. Compost, manure, residues from what the farm already produces. The plant is fed by the biology; feed the biology and the plant follows.

If a farm does those five things, it is regenerating, whatever anyone calls it. If it skips them, no label fixes it.

What each practice replaces on a real farm

The practices are easiest to understand by what they replace. On our farm in Laguna, each one took over a purchased input or a chore the old way demanded:

  • Living roots replaced bare fallow between cash crops, which used to mean buying herbicide to keep the weeds down on empty ground.
  • Covered soil replaced hand-weeding as the main weed strategy, because mulch outcompetes germination instead of fighting the plant.
  • Minimal disturbance replaced routine tilling, which had become a twice-a-season chore that cost fuel and broke the soil's structure every time.
  • Diversity replaced the single-cash-crop year, which concentrated the family's income into one harvest and one market price.
  • Feeding the biology replaced bagged fertilizer as the default, using rice straw, manure, and residues the farm used to burn or haul away.

None of these five transitions cost money to start. Each one traded a recurring purchase for attention and timing, and that trade is the economics of the whole method.

The difference between regenerative and organic is not what you stop using. It is what you start building.

How it differs from organic, sustainable, and permaculture

People arriving at this page are usually asking one of three comparison questions, and the differences are real enough to answer plainly:

Organic

Organic is a list of things a farm stops doing: no synthetic fertilizer, no synthetic pesticide. A farm can be certified organic and still till heavily, still leave soil bare between crops, and still lose topsoil every season. Organic is about what is absent from the field. Regenerative is about what is present and improving in it. Many regenerative farms are organic; organic does not require a farm to regenerate anything.

Sustainable

Sustainable means "able to continue." A farm can be sustainable and merely stop the bleeding, holding soil and profit steady without rebuilding anything. Regenerative aims higher: soil that is better next year than this one. In practice the words overlap enough that the market uses them loosely; the difference is the direction, maintenance versus improvement.

Permaculture

Permaculture is a design philosophy: permanent agriculture laid out as systems of zones, sectors, and guilds. It shares regenerative agriculture's respect for diversity and biology, and on a home garden scale the two are hard to tell apart. The practical difference is the working farm: permaculture design guides what to build; regenerative practice, as we teach it, is the season-by-season work of keeping a producing farm's soil alive while it pays.

Why the definition matters more in the Philippines

The imported advice fails here for one reason: timing. Most regenerative agriculture writing is temperate. It assumes a spring and a fall, a winter dormancy, and a growing calendar built for four seasons. The Philippines has a wet season and a dry one, typhoons instead of snow, and rice paddies that behave like nothing in the textbook. A definition is only useful if the practices underneath it survive translation.

That translation is the difference between this page and the hundreds of others answering the same question. The five practices above are not adapted to the tropics as an afterthought; they are taught here from a farm that runs them, in both seasons, in Laguna.

A definition you cannot farm is a slogan. Ours is farmed, in both seasons, on the same land for three generations.

What regenerative agriculture is not

Equal and opposite to the definition, and just as useful:

  • It is not a product. No spray, bottle, or bag makes a farm regenerative. Companies sell inputs; the practices above are mostly about needing fewer of them.
  • It is not a certificate. No certifier owns the term yet. When one arrives, it will describe a farm that already regenerates; it will not make one.
  • It is not an all-or-nothing conversion. A farm transitions one field, one season at a time. The first cover crop on one paddies' edge is a start, not a failure to convert everything at once.
  • It is not anti-farmer. Conventional farming is what most farmers know, and it fed this family for generations before we changed. The transition is a business decision made under cost pressure, not a moral one, and the farmers who make it deserve the economics explained, not a lecture.

Where the word came from

The term entered farming through soil science and the grazing world, and it spread because it described something the older words did not: not merely keeping a farm going, but leaving the land better than it was found. That is also why it is worth defining carefully. A word that means "improving" can be claimed by anyone, and the only honest test of a claim is whether the soil under it is getting better, season after season, in a way a farmer can see.

Frequently asked questions

What is regenerative agriculture in one sentence?

It is farming that rebuilds soil health instead of depleting it, through living roots, covered soil, minimal disturbance, crop diversity, and feeding the soil biology rather than only the plant.

Is regenerative agriculture the same as organic?

No. Organic stops using synthetic inputs; regenerative builds soil while doing it. A farm can be certified organic and still lose topsoil every season. Many regenerative farms are organic, but organic certification alone does not require regeneration.

Can a small farm in the Philippines do this?

Yes, and it is often easier than on a large one. The method is built around inputs the farm already produces, rice straw, manure, crop residues and on-farm seed, which lowers cost rather than raising it, and it is timed to the wet and dry seasons rather than a temperate calendar.

Does regenerative agriculture cost more to start?

Usually less. Most of the five practices replace purchased inputs with things the farm already grows or throws away. The real cost is attention and timing, which is what the course teaches, and the first buyers come in at a Founding Farmer rate in exchange for honest feedback from their own land.
