Measuring progress

If you did not write it down, you cannot prove it happened.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerYou measure regenerative farming with a notebook and four tests, not a laboratory: a worm count, a jar test, a water infiltration test, and the smell and crumb of a handful of soil, written down with dates season by season. Add your input costs in the same notebook, and the trend, not a single number, tells you whether the farm is improving.

Why measurement is the module that keeps farmers in the transition

The regenerative transition has an uncomfortable first season and a rewarding second one, and the difference between the farmers who get to the second and the ones who quit is almost always measurement. A farmer who wrote down the worm count, the infiltration time, and the input costs in the first season has something to compare against in the second. A farmer who remembers a bad first season, and nothing else, quits on a feeling.

Measurement is also what makes the whole method honest. Claims about soil health are easy to make and hard to prove, and the reason this site can say "the soil improves" is that the improvement is written down, season by season, in a notebook that costs nothing. A farm that measures can sell on its practice. A farm that guesses cannot.

The four tests, and how to run them

Each costs nothing, each takes minutes, and each is repeated in the same spot at the same time of season so the trend is real:

  1. Run the worm count. Dig a spade of soil, 20 centimeters deep, and count the earthworms in it. Do it in the same field, the same month, every season. Zero is a warning, two or three per spade is workable, eight or more is a working soil. Earthworms are the visible face of the soil biology: where they thrive, the rest of the biology is thriving too.
  2. Run the jar test. Fill a clear jar a third full with soil, top it with water, shake it hard, and leave it overnight. Sand settles first, then silt, then clay on top. Read the layers: they tell you how your soil drains, what it holds, and what your beds need. Do it once per field; the soil type does not change, but knowing it changes everything else you decide.
  3. Run the water infiltration test. Push an open-ended can about ten centimeters into the soil, pour in a measured amount of water, and time how long it takes to disappear. Fast is good: it means water is moving down through open channels. Slow is compaction, and compaction is what the cover crops and worms fix. Time it in the same spot each season.
  4. Read the smell and the crumb. Take a handful from spade depth. Healthy soil smells like a forest floor, dark and sweet. Degraded soil smells flat, sour, or like nothing at all. Squeeze it: healthy soil crumbles into crumbs when you open your hand; degraded soil shatters into dust or packs into a hard lump. This is the fastest test and the one to teach first.

Four lines in the notebook per field per season: worm count, infiltration time, smell, crumb. Written with the date and the weather. That record, kept for three years, is worth more to the farm than any single expensive analysis, because it is a trend rather than a snapshot.

Four lines per field per season, written down. That notebook is the difference between farming and guessing.

The cost notebook: the other half of the record

Soil health is half the measurement. The other half is money, because the transition has to pay for itself or it does not survive the neighbors' opinion. In the same notebook:

  • Input costs per season, in your own currency. Fertilizer, pesticide, seed, fuel for the tiller. Written as spent, not estimated. The regenerative transition shows up here first: compost and biofertilizer replace purchases, the tiller idles, and the numbers fall.
  • Labor per task. Hours spent weeding, watering, and tilling. This is where the transition pays second: a mulched, covered farm needs fewer hours of weeding and watering than a bare one.
  • What you harvested, and where it went. The yield, honestly, season by season. The transition's first season is often flat or slightly down; the second and third are where the biology's work shows. Without the record, the flat first season is all a farmer remembers.

The cost notebook is also what makes the economics module real instead of theoretical. The farmer who can show their own numbers falling while their soil improves is the farmer who convinces the cooperative, the buyer, and the neighbor.

Honest timelines for what shows when

  • Within one season: the infiltration test and worm count start moving, because the cover crop is feeding the biology immediately. The smell follows. Weeds drop where the mulch went down.
  • Within a year: the crumb structure, visibly better water holding after rain, and the first real drop in input costs.
  • Year two to three: the topsoil darkens, the beds hold structure between seasons, and the yield stabilizes above the old average in good years and below it in fewer bad ones.

And the honest failure mode: if the numbers are flat after three honest seasons, something in the system is missing. Usually it is the cover layer, or the compost is not actually being made, or the soil was more degraded than the transition assumed. The record is what tells you which, and that is the point of keeping it.

What to do when results do not show

Three questions, in order. First: is the cover actually on the soil, all of it, all year? A partial cover gives partial results, and the gaps show up in the worm count first. Second: is the compost pile actually running, or did it stop in the wet season and never restart? Third: was the starting point more degraded than the transition assumed, in which case the timeline stretches but the direction does not change. The notebook answers all three, because it holds the record of what was actually done rather than what was intended.

The notebook on our farm

The notebook is worth describing concretely, because its plainness is the point. It is a school exercise book, one page per field per season, in the father's handwriting. Each page has four lines: worms per spade, how long the water took, what it smelled like, what the crumb did. Below them: what was spent on inputs, what was harvested, and what the weather did that season.

What that record changed: the barangay Farmers Association meetings went from opinion to evidence. Farmers comparing pages can see whose fields are improving and ask what they did differently, which is how the method actually spreads, farmer to farmer, page to page. And the farm's own decisions stopped being arguments with memory: the numbers settle whether the compost, the mulch, or the cover made the difference, because memory edits itself and paper does not.

The kit being designed for first-season farmers includes this notebook as one of its items, for exactly that reason: it is the cheapest thing on the farm and the one that makes every other change provable.

How it connects to the rest of the method

Measurement is module ten of ten, the last one, because it is the one that never stops. It uses the tests introduced in knowing your soil, it makes the economics real, and it is what lets a farm sell on verified practice, which the course teaches. The first-season plan is where the notebook starts.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure soil health without a laboratory?

Four tests with household items: a worm count per spade, a jar test for soil type, a water infiltration test with an open can, and the smell and crumb of a handful from spade depth. Written down with dates in the same spot each season, they give a trend that is worth more than any single analysis.

How long before I see results in the soil?

The worm count and infiltration move within one season, the smell and crumb within a year, and the topsoil's visible darkening takes two to three years. The input costs fall first, often in the first year, which is what keeps most farmers in the transition long enough for the soil to answer.

What if my numbers are not improving?

Three checks, in order: is the cover on all the soil all year, is the compost pile actually running, and was the starting point more degraded than assumed. The notebook answers all three, because it holds what was actually done rather than what was intended. Flat numbers after three honest seasons mean something in the system is missing.

Do I need to track money as well as soil?

Yes, in the same notebook. Input costs, labor hours, and harvests per season. The transition pays in the budget before it pays in the soil test, and the farmer who can show both moving is the one who convinces a cooperative, a buyer, or a neighbor.
