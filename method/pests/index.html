Pests without poisons

A pest problem is usually a system problem wearing a bug's face.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerRegenerative farming does not use routine pesticide spraying. It prevents most pest problems instead: diverse crops, covered soil, and healthy biology grow stronger plants and feed the insects that eat the pests. When an outbreak still arrives, the response is scouting, then targeted homemade sprays, and only rarely a targeted intervention, used as a decision rather than a habit.

So does regenerative farming use pesticides?

The honest answer: not routinely, and not as the system's tool. A regenerative farm's pest management is prevention first, and the prevention is structural. When an outbreak still breaks through, the response escalates in steps: handpicking and physical removal, then sprays made from farm materials, and in a genuine emergency a targeted intervention on one affected area. What it never reverts to is the calendar spray, the prophylactic routine that exists because it always has.

That distinction matters because the question is usually asked by a farmer deciding whether to change, and the honest framing is this: a regenerative farm is not a farm that never loses anything to pests. It is a farm where pest losses are the exception rather than the plan, where the response starts with something cheaper than a bottle, and where the system itself does most of the defending.

Why a regenerative system gets fewer pest problems

Four mechanisms, all of them consequences of practices described in other modules:

  • Diversity starves a specialist. A monoculture is an unlimited buffet for the one insect that eats that crop. A farm growing many crops, in rows and in layers, means no pest finds an unbroken meal. This is the single biggest mechanism and it comes from diverse planting.
  • Healthy plants defend themselves. A plant fed by living biology builds tougher cell walls, thicker leaf surfaces, and its own chemical defenses. A plant force-fed soluble nitrogen grows soft, fast tissue that sucking insects prefer. This comes from compost and biofertilizer.
  • The pests have enemies. Ladybugs, lacewings, spiders, parasitic wasps, birds, and frogs eat pest insects, and they live where there is habitat: hedgerows, cover, mulch, and flowers. A farm with habitat has pest control living on it for free. A bare monoculture has nowhere for them to stay.
  • Healthy soil grows resilient roots. Stressed plants from compacted, waterlogged, or dead soil are the first to attract pests. Much of what looks like a pest problem starts as a root problem. This comes from the soil work.
A pest outbreak on a diverse farm is an event. On a monoculture it is the business model.

What to do when pests still arrive

They will still arrive. The escalation that works, in order:

  1. Scout first, every time. Walk the affected area and identify what is actually eating what. Most damage is from a handful of culprits, and each has a different response. Spraying before identifying is the habit the method removes.
  2. Handpick and remove. On a small farm this is genuinely practical for the big culprits: beetles, caterpillars, snails, egg clusters under leaves. A morning's picking beats a season's spraying for the pests it catches.
  3. Homemade sprays from farm materials. The classics that work: chili-garlic-soap sprays against soft-bodied insects, neem where the tree is available against chewing and sucking pests, wood ash as a barrier against some crawlers. These are contact tools: they work where they land, they break down fast, and they do not leave residue on the crop.
  4. Water and timing. A strong jet of water removes aphid colonies. Spraying in the evening, when pollinators are not flying, protects the insects you want to keep.
  5. A targeted intervention, rarely, as a decision. A genuine outbreak threatening a season's work is sometimes answered with a specific, targeted treatment on one affected area, chosen because the alternative is losing the crop. What makes that a decision rather than a habit is that it is rare, targeted, and followed by asking why the outbreak happened, which is usually a system answer: too much of one crop, stressed plants, or habitat that disappeared.

Weeds: indicators first, enemies second

Weeds get the same treatment as insects: understand what they are saying before fighting them. Weeds are honest indicators of soil condition, and a farmer who reads them learns their fields faster than any test:

  • Compacted, wet ground grows the weeds that tolerate it. Patches of them in a field point at a drainage or compaction problem before the crop shows it.
  • Bare, depleted ground grows fast, short-lived pioneer weeds whose job is covering exposed soil. They are the soil's emergency response, and they are telling you the farm needs a planted cover instead.
  • Fertile, healthy ground still grows weeds, but different ones, and slower. The weed pressure drops as the cover crops and mulch take over the space the weeds were filling.

Practically: weeds are managed by competition and cover, the same as insect pests are managed by habitat and diversity. A thick mulch layer, the cover crops, and never leaving ground bare do more for weeds than any spray, because they remove the space the weeds were colonizing.

Building the habitat that holds the good insects

The pest predators are only resident if the farm has somewhere for them to live. Habitat is the part of pest control that costs nothing and gets skipped most often:

  • Hedgerows and boundary plantings. The kakawate and madre de cacao on the fence lines are not just green manure: they are perches for birds, cover for spiders and frogs, and nectar for the parasitic wasps that lay their eggs in caterpillars. Every boundary planting is pest control standing still.
  • Flowers through the seasons. The adults of many pest-eating insects drink nectar; their larvae eat the pests. A farm with something flowering year round keeps the adults resident, which means their larvae are on station when the pests arrive.
  • Mulch and groundcover. Ground beetles, spiders, and frogs live under the straw layer. A bare farm has no ambush predators; a mulched one has them everywhere.
  • A water source. Even a small pond or a wet ditch keeps frogs resident, and frogs are among the best pest control a tropical farm has.

On our farm, the chickens and goats are part of this too, managed so they clean up fallen fruit and scratch the ground behind the animals' rotation without touching the crops. The animals are a layer of the system, and pest cleanup is one of their jobs.

Disease: the same answer with a longer fuse

Fungal and bacterial disease follows the same logic: prevention is structural, and the structure is air, light, and dry leaves. Crowded plantings hold humidity on leaf surfaces; the tropics grow fungus fast in still, wet air. Spaced plantings, pruned canopies, and morning irrigation (so leaves dry in the day rather than staying wet overnight) prevent most of it. Diseased material is removed and composted hot rather than left to spread, and rotation keeps soil-borne disease from building up in one bed.

Frequently asked questions

Does regenerative farming use pesticides at all?

Not routinely. Prevention is the tool: diversity, healthy plants, and habitat for pest predators. When an outbreak still arrives, the response starts with handpicking and homemade sprays from farm materials, and a targeted intervention is a rare, deliberate decision on one affected area rather than a seasonal routine.

What is the best homemade pest spray?

Chili, garlic, and soap blended and strained in water is the workhorse against soft-bodied insects, and neem where the tree grows covers chewing and sucking pests. They are contact sprays: they work where they land and break down fast, so they are applied to the pest, in the evening, rather than across the whole farm.

Will I lose more of my crop to pests without spraying?

In the transition seasons, possibly, while the habitat and diversity establish. Once the system is running, most farms see pest losses become the exception rather than the plan, because the mechanisms that prevent outbreaks are structural: no unbroken buffet for specialists, tougher plants, and predators living on the farm.

What do weeds tell you about your soil?

Weeds are indicators: patches of wet-ground weeds point at drainage or compaction problems, pioneer weeds on bare ground point at depleted soil that needs a planted cover, and the overall weed pressure drops as mulch and cover crops take over the space. Read them before fighting them.
