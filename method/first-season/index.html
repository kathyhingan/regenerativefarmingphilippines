Your first season

How to start without buying your way in or breaking your farm.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerStart regenerative farming by observing before changing anything, then in order: cover every patch of bare soil, start one compost pile, stop tilling one field, and write everything down. Skip the big purchases, the whole-farm conversion, and the premium market plans in year one. The first season builds the system; the harvest it improves comes in the seasons after.

What the first season is actually for

The first season of regenerative farming is not about proving the method works. It is about building the system that makes every season after it work: the cover on the soil, the compost pile running, the notebook started, and one field where you have watched the biology come back. A farmer who spends year one on those four things enters year two with a running system. A farmer who spends year one trying to convert everything at once, buying inputs and chasing a premium market, usually quits by the second, because nothing was ever measured and nothing was ever allowed to establish.

The steps below are in the order they actually happen on a tropical smallholding, and each one is deliberately small. The method's first season is a sequence of cheap, reversible decisions, not a leap.

The first-season steps

  1. Walk the land and change nothing, for the first weeks. Note where water stands after rain, where the soil is bare or crusted, what weeds grow where, what already grows well, and where the wind and the sun hit hardest. Write it down. The urge to act immediately is the first beginner mistake: every change you make before observing is a guess about a farm you have not yet read.
  2. Cover every patch of bare soil. This is the first real action and the cheapest: rice straw, crop residue, or a fast cover crop like munggo, over every exposed patch. Bare soil is losing moisture, biology, and topsoil every day it sits exposed. Covering it is the single highest-impact change on the farm and it costs almost nothing.
  3. Start one compost pile. Small, about a cubic meter, from materials the farm already produces: straw layered with manure, watered as you build, turned when the center cools. One pile, not a composting operation. It teaches the balance cheaply and it is running by the time you need it.
  4. Start a biofertilizer brew. Once the first pile is going, a container of fermented plant material and manure gives the farm its liquid fertility for the season. Kakawate or madre de cacao leaves, weeds, manure, molasses, water, weeks of fermentation. Small batch, from the farm's own boundaries.
  5. Pick one field and stop tilling it. The best-soiled or smallest field, covered first, is the test. Hand-open the planting holes, keep the mulch on, and let the cover crop run between crops. The rest of the farm keeps its old routine while you learn what the transition looks like on your own ground.
  6. Plant the cover on the gaps. Between cash crops, on paddies' edges, under trees: something growing everywhere the cash crop is not. This is the layer that keeps the biology fed and the weeds out, and it is the difference between a covered farm and a regenerating one.
  7. Write down the soil tests and the costs. Worm count, infiltration, smell, crumb: one line each, per field, with the date. Input costs and harvest in the same notebook. This is the step that makes everything else provable, and it is the one skipped most often.
  8. Watch the wet season. If the first season includes the rains: the covered ground holds, the bare ground washes, and the difference is the most convincing argument on the farm. Check the drainage before the typhoons, not after.
  9. Read the results honestly. At the season's end, the notebook tells you what moved: worms, water, weeds, costs. Mostly flat numbers after one season are normal; the direction is what matters, and year two is where it compounds.
The first season builds the system. The system builds the farm.

What to skip in year one

Just as important as the steps, because these are the mistakes that cost a year:

  • Big purchases. No equipment, no amendment supply, no brewing hardware beyond a container. Every regenerative practice in the first season runs on things the farm already has. The farm that buys its way in learns nothing and spends what the transition was supposed to save.
  • The whole-farm conversion. Stopping tillage everywhere, covering everything, and rebuilding all at once produces a weed flush, a workload shock, and a discouraged farmer. One field, done properly, converts the farmer; the farmer converts the farm.
  • The premium market plan. Selling a premium crop requires a consistent harvest, which requires a running system, which takes seasons. Planning the market before the system produces is how farms end up promising what they cannot fill.
  • Chasing every practice at once. The seven layers, five crops, compost, brews, and measurement all in year one means none of them established. Two or three done properly beat nine attempted.
  • Judging by the first harvest. The first season's numbers are usually flat. The farmers who quit do it on year one's feelings; the ones who stay do it on year one's direction, which the notebook holds.

The classic beginner mistakes, and the fix

  • Tilling the mulch in. The straw layer is doing its job on the surface; burying it restarts the damage. Feed from the top.
  • Cover crops gone to seed. One uncut strip of crotalaria becomes next season's weed problem. Mark the cutting date when you sow.
  • Compost that stopped. A pile left to dry out or waterlog in the rains goes cold and stays there. Walk it every few days; water it in the dry, cover it in the wet.
  • Spraying before scouting. The calendar spray habit survives the transition longer than any tiller. Identify first; the pests module covers the escalation.
  • Expecting the neighbors to approve. A mulched field looks abandoned to a barangay of bare-tilled farms. The second season's field is the answer, and the notebook is the proof.

The first season on our farm, as it happened

The order above is not theory; it is the order the transition actually ran on our own ground, and the parts that did not go to plan are the useful part. The first mulch went on late, after the first hot weeks had already baked the beds, which is how we learned that covering comes before anything else. The first compost pile sat cold for a month because it was all straw and no manure, which is how the balance became a felt thing rather than a recipe. The first field without tillage crusted and drew comments from every neighbor, which is how we learned that the cover had to be thick enough to answer them.

What went right was smaller than expected and more important: the worms came back in the test field within the first season, the straw stopped being burned, and the notebook, in the father's handwriting, started holding what the farm was actually doing. That notebook is now the record the whole method is taught from, and it started as the least impressive thing on the farm.

The first season did not prove the method. It built the system that proved it in the seasons after, which is exactly what a first season is for.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start regenerative farming on a small budget?

With what the farm already produces: cover the bare soil with rice straw or crop residue, start one compost pile from straw and manure, plant a packet of munggo on the gaps, and write everything down. The first season's only purchase is a packet of seed, and the transition was designed so that is enough.

How much land do I need to start?

One field, one bed, or one backyard plot. The method works at any scale and proves itself fastest at small scale, where one person can cover the soil, watch the pile, and read the results without help. Start with the smallest piece of ground you control and let it convert the rest.

How long before a regenerative farm becomes profitable?

The costs fall first, often in the first year, as compost and residues replace purchases and the tiller idles. The yields stabilize and climb in the second and third seasons as the biology establishes. Anyone promising a first-season profit is guessing; the honest answer is that the budget improves before the harvest does.

What should I NOT do in my first season?

Three things: do not buy equipment or inputs, do not convert the whole farm at once, and do not plan a premium market before the system produces consistently. The first season is for building the system on one field with what the farm already has, and for starting the notebook that proves it worked.
