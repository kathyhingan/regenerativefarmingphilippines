Cover crops for regenerative farming

Something growing, all year. Even where nothing pays.

By the farmer at Viking Farms PH2 October 2026

Short answerA cover crop is something you grow to feed the soil rather than to sell. On a tropical farm it keeps living roots in the ground between cash crops, holds moisture, smothers weeds, and adds nitrogen for free when you cut it. Legumes like mung bean and cowpea are the cheapest start, and rice straw mulch does the same job without planting.

What a cover crop actually does

A cover crop is a plant grown for the soil's benefit rather than for harvest. Between cash crops, on paddies' edges, under trees, and on any ground that would otherwise sit bare, it does four jobs at once:

  • It keeps a living root in the ground. Roots are the food supply for the soil biology, delivered underground where that biology lives. Bare soil starves it; a growing cover feeds it every day it is in the ground.
  • It covers the surface. Leaves intercept the rain before it hammers bare ground and shades the soil before the sun bakes it. Covered soil loses less moisture in the dry season and less topsoil in the wet one.
  • It competes with weeds. A thick cover occupies the space, light, and nutrients a weed seedling needs. Suppressing germination is cheaper than fighting an established weed.
  • Legumes add nitrogen. Mung bean, cowpea, and their relatives host bacteria in their roots that pull nitrogen from the air. Cut the crop at flowering and that nitrogen is in the soil, for the price of a packet of seed.

Those four jobs are why cover cropping is the fastest of the regenerative practices to prove itself. The soil responds within a single season, because the roots and the cover are working immediately.

The species that work in the Philippines

The imported guides list species that either do not grow here or behave differently in a wet and dry season cycle. These are the ones that work on tropical ground, and on our farm:

Legumes, the nitrogen builders

  • Mung bean (munggo). Fast, cheap, familiar to every Filipino farmer, and it fixes nitrogen well. Sow it between rice crops or on fallow paddies, cut it at first flowering, and leave the residue where it falls.
  • Cowpea. Tougher than munggo in dry conditions, deep-rooted, and it covers ground fast. The seed is edible, so a farmer can let a strip go to harvest and still use the rest as a cover.
  • Pigeon pea (kadyos). A perennial legume that doubles as food. It grows tall enough to shade out weeds on edges and boundaries, and it keeps producing for years with no replanting.
  • Crotalaria. Less familiar, but the fastest biomass builder of the set, and it suppresses certain root nematodes. Cut it before it seeds or it will volunteer everywhere.

Grasses and residues, the soil holders

  • Rice straw mulch. Not planted at all: the previous crop's residue laid over the beds. It is the cheapest cover on any rice farm because it already exists, it suppresses weeds, holds moisture, and breaks down into the same organic matter a cover crop would have made. On our farm, straw used to be burned. Now it is the default mulch on every bed.
  • Napier and other grasses. On boundaries and slopes, grass strips hold soil against the wet season's rain and give cut fodder for animals. Their root systems are deep and fibrous, which makes them the best erosion stoppers of the set.
  • Kakawate and madre de cacao. Not ground covers but tree legumes whose leaves make the classic Philippine green manure. Cut the branches, wilt the leaves, and lay them over beds or chop them into compost. Every old farm boundary here already has them planted.
The cheapest cover crop on a rice farm is the one already in the field: the straw.

When to cut, and why timing decides everything

A cover crop cut too late becomes a weed. Cut too early and it has not built the nitrogen or the biomass. The rule that works:

  • Cut legumes at first flowering. That is the moment the nitrogen in their roots peaks. Flowering also marks the point where the plant starts putting energy into seed rather than into roots, so every day after first flowering the soil gains less.
  • Cut grasses and non-legumes before they seed. Once a grass puts up a seed head, the cover becomes next season's weed problem. Cut it while it is still vegetative and leave the residue on the surface.
  • Leave everything where it falls. The residue is the point. Cutting and removing a cover crop gives the farm nothing but the work; cutting and leaving it gives the soil the nitrogen, the organic matter, and the mulch layer in one motion.
  • Never let a cover crop compete with a cash crop. On beds, plant covers between seasons, not beside a growing cash crop. Under trees, keep a ring clear around each trunk and let the cover run to the drip line.

The seven layers: where covers sit in the whole system

A cover crop is one layer of seven on a regenerative tropical farm. The full planting, from top to bottom: canopy trees, vines, cash crops, cover crops, roots, groundcover, and animals in the same system. The layers stack because each one uses a different slice of the light, the space, and the soil depth, which is how a small farm ends up producing more than its area says it should.

On our farm the vine layer is dragonfruit, the cash crop that pays while the other layers build soil. The cover layer feeds everything under it. A farmer does not need all seven layers in the first season; the cover layer is the one to establish first, because it is the cheapest and it makes every layer above it cheaper to run.

What a cover costs, and what it replaces

The economics are the reason this practice comes early in the method. A packet of munggo seed costs less than one bottle of herbicide, and it replaces the herbicide's job plus three others. The comparison that decides it:

  • Against herbicide: a cover suppresses germination every day it grows, for one purchase, instead of a spray per flush.
  • Against bought fertilizer: a legume cut at flowering adds nitrogen and biomass for the price of seed, against a bag of urea per season.
  • Against irrigation: a mulch layer or cover holds moisture through the dry weeks, which is water and fuel the farm does not spend.
  • Against labor: weeding is one of the biggest labor costs on a small farm. A thick cover removes most of the germination before it becomes a hoe's worth of work.

None of those replacements is a compromise. Each one is the same job done by a plant instead of a purchase, which is why the transition usually pays for itself in the first season it is tried honestly.

Common mistakes, and the fix

  • Letting a cover go to seed. One season of uncut crotalaria becomes three seasons of volunteer crotalaria. The fix is a calendar: mark the cutting date when you sow, and walk the strip weekly as flowering starts.
  • Cutting and removing the residue. A removed cover crop is work for nothing. Always leave the biomass on the surface where it grew.
  • Planting a single species everywhere. One species feeds one pest and uses one soil slice. Mixing a legume with a grass covers both jobs and hedges the failure of either.
  • Expecting a cover crop to fix compacted ground alone. Compaction needs a deep root plus time; a shallow-rooted cover helps but will not break a hardpan. Pair it with the minimal disturbance approach and give it two seasons.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best cover crop for a tropical farm?

Mung bean is the best first cover crop for most Philippine farms: it is cheap, fast, fixes nitrogen, and every farmer already knows how to grow it. Cowpea for drier ground, pigeon pea for boundaries, and rice straw mulch wherever a crop's residue already exists.

When should I cut a cover crop?

Cut legumes at first flowering, when the nitrogen in their roots peaks, and cut grasses before they put up a seed head. Leave all the residue where it falls. A cover crop left uncut past flowering becomes next season's weed.

Do cover crops steal water from my cash crop?

Not when they are managed as covers rather than competitors. Plant them between cash crop seasons or to the drip line of trees, keep a clear ring around each crop, and cut them before the cash crop's water demand peaks. A mulch layer holds more moisture than it uses.

Can I use rice straw as mulch instead of planting a cover?

Yes, and on a rice farm it is the cheapest start. Straw mulch covers the soil, suppresses weeds, holds moisture, and breaks down into organic matter. It does not add living roots while it sits, so pair it with a legume cover on the next gap for the full effect.
