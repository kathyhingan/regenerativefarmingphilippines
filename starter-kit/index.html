The starter kit

A first season in a box. Being built now.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerThe starter kit is the first season in a box, and it is being built now. It is designed to cover the five things a first season needs: hand tools, cover crop and vegetable seed, compost and biofertilizer starter materials, a season planner and notebook, and the companion first-season guide. Contents and pricing are announced to the waitlist first, and the kit works with what the farm already produces, not instead of it.

What the kit is

The starter kit is being designed around one principle: a farmer's first regenerative season should not start with shopping. The method's first season runs on what the farm already produces, and the kit exists for the gap between that and what a farm actually has: the cover crop seed the farm does not grow yet, the compost starter materials if the animals or the straw are missing, the notebook that makes the season provable.

It is the companion to the first-season plan, not a substitute for it. The plan is the method; the kit is what the plan says to have on hand. A farmer who buys the kit without doing the first season has bought a box; a farmer who does the first season with the kit has a running start.

The five categories the kit is being designed to cover

These are the categories, each with the reason it belongs. The specific items and quantities are being finalized, and they are announced to the waitlist first:

  1. Hand tools. The tools a minimal-disturbance first season actually uses: a hoe or spade for opening planting holes and strips by hand, and little else. The method's first season deliberately does not need machinery, and the kit is designed so the tools it carries are the ones the season asks for, not a shed full.
  2. Cover crop and vegetable seed. The packet of munggo or cowpea that starts the cover layer, plus the vegetable seed for the first season's cash crop. This is the one purchase the first-season plan genuinely requires, and the kit is being designed so the seed is tropical-appropriate rather than whatever a generic catalog ships.
  3. Compost and biofertilizer starter materials. For the farm whose animals or straw are not yet producing what the compost pile needs: the starter materials that get the first pile running, and the brewing container for the biofertilizer. A farm with straw and manure already on the property needs none of this, which is the method's whole point; the kit covers the farms that are starting from a bare piece of ground.
  4. A season planner and the measurement notebook. The notebook is the single cheapest item on the farm and the one that makes every other change provable: the four soil tests with dates, the input costs, the harvest. The season planner carries the tropical wet and dry calendar, not a temperate one, because timing is where imported advice fails here.
  5. The companion first-season guide. The written walkthrough of the first season, mapped to the kit's contents: what to do in what order, what to skip, and how to read the results. The guide is the kit's instruction layer, and it is the same first-season plan taught in the course.
The kit exists for the gap between what a farm has and what a first season needs. The farm's own materials come first; the kit covers the rest.

What the kit is not

Three things, stated plainly, because they are the difference between this kit and the "farm in a box" products on the market:

  • It is not a substitute for the method. No box makes a farm regenerative. The practices do, and they run on what the farm already produces. The kit is the companion; the course is the method.
  • It is not a load of equipment. The first season needs hand tools, not machinery. The kit is designed to be carried, not delivered on a truck, because the method's first season is deliberately small.
  • It is not a complete substitute for the farm's own materials. The compost module's whole argument is that the farm's straw, manure, and residues are the real fertility. The kit covers what the farm does not have yet; it does not replace what it does.

Who the kit is for

Three situations, and the kit fits each differently:

  • The backyard or small-plot beginner starting from a bare piece of ground: the kit covers what the ground does not have yet, seed and starter materials, and the planner makes the season provable from day one.
  • The smallholder with a running farm starting the transition on one field: the farm already has the straw and the manure, so the kit's seed, planner, and guide are the pieces that matter. The compost materials can be skipped.
  • The farmer who wants everything in one place rather than sourcing a packet here and a container there: the kit exists for exactly this, and the tradeoff is stated plainly, it costs more than sourcing it yourself and saves the runs.

When it ships, and how to get it

The kit is being built alongside the course, and the contents and pricing are announced to the waitlist first: the people on the list see what is in it, what it costs, and when it is ready, before anyone else. The waitlist is free, and the first guides for your crop go out to it as they are finished.

The Founding Farmer rate applies to the first twenty-five buyers when the doors open: fifty percent off the course tier, a place on the Founding Farmers page, and a request, never a requirement, for honest feedback from their own land after the first season. That feedback is what the proof assets of this site are made of, and it is why the rate exists.

What the first season looks like with the kit

Worth spelling out, because the kit only makes sense in the sequence. The ground is covered first: the straw from the last crop, or the cover crop seed from the kit, over every bare patch. The compost pile starts: from the farm's straw and manure, or from the kit's starter materials on a farm that has neither yet. The planner goes up on the wall with the tropical calendar marked, and the notebook starts holding the four soil tests and the costs. The hand tool opens the first planting holes. The guide sits open to the first-season plan, and the season runs in the order it lays out.

Nothing in that sequence is a big purchase, and the kit's role in it is smaller than a box of tools suggests: it fills the gaps the farm does not have yet and removes the sourcing runs. The fertility comes from the farm. The method comes from the course. The kit is the third piece, and it is the one that exists only because the first two need a start.

Why the kit exists at all

Worth answering directly, because the honest question is why a method built on "use what the farm already produces" sells a box. The answer: the method's first season needs almost nothing, but it needs three specific things a bare farm does not have, the cover crop seed, the compost starter materials, and the planner that makes the season provable. Sourcing those three takes the runs, and the runs are the friction that stops more first seasons than any agronomic problem. The kit removes the friction; the farm supplies the fertility. Both halves are needed, and neither replaces the other.
