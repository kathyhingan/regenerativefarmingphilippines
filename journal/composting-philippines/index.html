Composting in the Philippines: turning farm waste into fertility

Rice straw, manure, and crop waste are the cheapest inputs a Philippine farm will ever hold. This is how we turn ours into fertility instead of smoke.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski25 October 2026

Short answerComposting in the Philippines is mostly a farm waste question: rice straw, manure, and crop residues that too often end up burned. Here is the pile method that works in tropical heat and rain, the in-field option for rice land, and what the finished compost replaces on the purchase list.

The waste problem, in numbers anyone can check

Every rice-producing country has a straw problem, and the Philippines has a big one. The RiceStrawPH project that IRRI runs with Philippine research partners puts the country's rice crop at 15.2 million tons a year, which leaves about 11.3 million tons of straw spread across the fields, and counts 60 to 80 percent of it burned in the open field. Anyone who has driven through Laguna or Nueva Ecija in the weeks after harvest has seen the season's real crop map: it is written in smoke.

Burning feels free and settles the field in an afternoon, which is why it wins by default. The costs arrive elsewhere, unitemized: the fertility that goes up with the smoke, the neighbors downwind, and the fertilizer bill that lands every season because the free fertility was never captured. A statement by the EcoWaste Coalition put the standard chemical fertilizer at 800 to 1,000 pesos a bag and made the obvious point in the other direction: straw composted is fertilizer the farm did not have to buy. Their statement also noted that at least one province, Leyte, has passed an ordinance banning the open burning of rice straw, so the default is quietly becoming a liability as well.

None of this requires a new technology or a subsidy. It requires the pile. The rest of this guide is the pile, built the way a small Philippine farm actually builds it: with what the lot already grows, in a climate that adds water half the year and takes it away the other half. Every purchase line the compost deletes is arithmetic the making it pay module keeps honest.

What the government already teaches, and what it skips

The country is not short of composting instruction. The Department of Agriculture's extension arm publishes plain guides to simple composting, and the Bureau of Soils and Water Management maintains the modified rapid composting protocol for rice land. The national agricultural engineering standard for composting, PAES 414-2, fixes the technical bands: a carbon to nitrogen ratio between 25:1 and 40:1, moisture held at 50 to 60 percent. These documents are good, they are free, and every figure we quote later comes from them or from IRRI's straw management work.

What the documents skip is the family scale and the weather. The extension PDFs describe the pile but rarely the sequence around it: where it sits in a wet season, what to do the week the straw comes off the field, how a household with two shovels and a carabao times the work, and what a sour-smelling failure teaches before the second attempt. The rapid composting protocol was written for irrigated rice areas such as Talavera in Nueva Ecija, where it has been practiced for years, and it assumes conditions a vegetable-and-livestock lot does not share.

So this guide runs both directions at once. It repeats the official figures where they are the truth, and it adds the parts the farm had to work out alone: the siting, the season, the failures, and the order of operations that keeps the pile from becoming one more abandoned project at the back of the lot. Readers who want the recipes in days and pesos will find them in the compost and biofertilizers module; this page is the field version of the same practice.

The browns and greens already on the farm

Composting is accounting before it is biology, and the first ledger is the inventory. Browns, the carbon-rich side of the 25:1 to 40:1 band, are what Philippine farms have in surplus: rice straw, sugarcane tops and trash after harvest, corn stover, dry leaves, rice hull, coconut husk and coir, sawdust, shredded cardboard from the market run. Greens, the nitrogen side, are scarcer and more valuable: animal manure of any kind, kitchen peelings, fresh weeds pulled before they seed, legume trimmings, and the slow compost of ordinary garden waste.

On our own lot in Calamba the ledger reads like the farm map. Two rice crops a year deliver the straw, the sugarcane delivers tops, the pens deliver manure mixed deep into straw bedding, and the kitchen delivers its daily bucket. That bedding trick is worth naming because it is the quiet engine of the whole system: straw spread thick in the pens soaks up manure all season, keeps the animals clean and dry, and comes out the far end as a half-composted brown-and-green blend that finishes fast in the windrow. The farm's two biggest waste streams arrive at the pile already mixed.

The extension guides put the working carbon to nitrogen target at about 30:1, and the practical rule they give is simpler than any ratio: alternate layers of brown and green, moistening as you go, until a squeezed handful holds together without dripping. The squeeze test is the moisture gauge the whole system runs on, and it replaces the laboratory the standard assumes. PAES 414-2 wants the pile held between 50 and 60 percent moisture, which the hand measures well enough that the numbers matter mainly for anyone writing a proposal rather than turning a heap.

The pile method, step by step at farm scale

The Department of Agriculture's simple composting guide lays out a sequence that survives translation to a working farm almost intact, and it is the skeleton of what we run:

  • Siting. Near the feedstock and near the beds it will feed, on ground that drains. In the wet half of the year, high ground is the difference between a pile and a swamp; the compost site guide is explicit that the site should sit near both the raw materials and the fields the compost will serve.
  • Size. Shred or chop what goes in, and build the pile at least a meter wide and a meter tall. The guide's reasoning is thermal: a smaller pile will not heat, and a much bigger one composts unevenly. A meter is also the honest limit of two shovels and a wheelbarrow, which is the equipment most families actually have.
  • Layering. Alternate browns and greens in layers, moistening each. A thin layer of garden soil or finished compost between layers seeds the microbes and works as well as anything sold in a bottle.
  • Activator. The guide suggests spraying a compost activator such as Trichoderma to hasten decomposition. It is optional and it buys weeks, which the next section prices honestly.
  • Cover. Outdoor piles get covered with plastic sheet, used sacks, or banana and coconut leaves. The cover keeps rain from leaching the nutrients out in the wet season and keeps moisture from escaping in the dry one. Our windrow spends October under sacks and April being watered, and the cover is doing one of those two jobs every day of the year.
  • Turning. The guide says turn daily for fast, uniform decomposition, and check moisture between turnings. Daily is the commercial tempo; a family pile turned every few days still gets there, just slower, and the home composting version of the same guide says exactly that.
  • Temperature. The folk test in the guide is the one we use: push a metal rod into the pile for a couple of hours, pull it, and hold it. Too hot to hold means the pile is working. The heat matters beyond proof: the same guide notes the pile's core, held at 50 to 70 degrees Celsius, kills weed seeds and pathogens that cooler piles carry forward.
  • Harvest. With an activator and daily turning, the guide calls compost harvestable in 2 to 4 weeks, and recommends broadcasting it as basal fertilizer before final harrowing. We side-dress beds and ring-feed perennials with it as well, and the coarse, unfinished tail of the pile goes back under the next one as seed and bulk.

The one instruction we would add is patience with the calendar rather than the pile. An activator pile finished in a month is a genuine result, and piles without activator or with a lazy turner take two to four months, which is compost all the same. The farm runs both speeds without drama: the fast pile feeds the planting schedule, the slow pile exists so that the fast one can keep its promises.

Three other methods that fit Philippine lots

The windrow is not the only shape compost takes here, and the right alternative depends on what the lot grows and who does the carrying.

In-field rapid composting for rice land. The Bureau of Soils and Water Management's modified rapid composting skips the pile entirely for rice paddies: the straw is scattered evenly in the field itself, soaked with a shallow irrigation, sprayed with a Trichoderma harzianum or effective microorganism solution, and given a broadcast of urea to hasten decomposition, with the field holding moisture for 15 to 20 days before normal land preparation resumes. The bureau designed it as a fertilizer cost reduction strategy for small rice farmers, and it converts the biggest waste stream on a rice farm without moving a single bundle. The trade is real: the nitrogen top-up is a purchase, and the nutrients stay in the paddy rather than concentrating where vegetables could use them.

Pit composting for yards and home gardens. The old Progressive Farming text in the UP repository describes the pit method most Filipino households half-remember: dig where shade and drainage agree, layer garden refuse with thin bands of animal dung, cover with soil, and wait about 4 to 5 months. It is the lowest-labor method in the list and the slowest, and it puts fertility exactly where a future planting will go, which is either clever or inflexible depending on how much you trust your own garden plan.

Basket pits in vegetable beds. The farm radio script method weaves a bamboo basket a hand-span across, sinks it into the raised bed, and keeps it fed with kitchen waste and manure; the bed's roots forage the pit's edges, and at harvest the pit's contents are spread and the basket moves to a new hole. It is composting as a habit rather than a project, it costs nothing but bamboo, and it is the natural next step for readers who came from the kitchen-scale gardening guide.

Vermicomposting deserves its own guide and will get one: IRRI's straw management handbook pegs it as the better fit for small to medium straw volumes where conditions can be kept stable, and the worms replace most of the turning labor with appetite. We run a starter bed ourselves, and we would rather report on it after a full year than print enthusiasm on day forty.

Timing in a two-season country

Textbook composting assumes seasons that alternate politely. A Philippine farm runs two different composting problems in the same year, and the guides written for four-season climates skip both. In the wet season, water is free and excessive: piles gain moisture they cannot use, nutrients leach downward, and an uncovered windrow can slide from working to sour after one week of rain. The countermeasures are the ones the extension guide names, cover and site selection, plus a farmer's reflex: after every heavy rain, walk the pile, check the squeeze, and turn if the core has gone cold and sodden.

In the dry season the problem inverts. Moisture leaves faster than the pile can hold it, and an unwatered pile stalls into slow, dusty decay. Watering the heap at turning is then a scheduled chore, not an afterthought, and the squeeze test becomes the daily instrument. The bureau's rapid composting protocol, built for irrigated rice areas, runs through both halves of the year, its practice seasons listed as wet from May to October and dry from December to April, and the same calendar works for pile composting everywhere the rains follow that curve.

The farm's own timing rule is simpler than any of it: build the big windrow at harvest. Straw is free, abundant, and dry exactly when the crop comes off, manure has been bedding all season, and the pile that starts in harvest week finishes in time to feed the planting that follows it. Every other date on the composting calendar is negotiable; that one pays.

What compost replaces

The direct replacement is the fertilizer purchase, and the arithmetic is the reason the pile exists. The EcoWaste Coalition's 800 to 1,000 pesos per bag is the price of the sack the compost displaces a few at a time, and on a mixed lot with two rice crops, cane, and vegetables, the straw and manure the farm already pays to handle become the input that trims that bill every season. The compost module carries the peso arithmetic in full, and the honest summary is that compost rarely deletes the fertilizer line in year one. It shrinks it, and the shrink is durable because the reason it works does not expire.

The indirect replacements compound. Compost is a slow-release feed that feeds the soil's biology rather than the plant directly, which is the difference between a dose and a diet, and the soil module follows that thread into structure, water holding, and the seasons a fed soil survives that a fertilized one does not. The Department of Agriculture's own balanced fertilization circulars make the same case from the government side: organic inputs first, synthetic topped up to match the soil test, and the pile is the organic half of that instruction.

What the pile really replaces, at the scale where most Philippine farms live, is the decision to burn. The loop is the one our integrated farming guide maps end to end: straw to bedding, bedding to manure, manure to windrow, windrow to bed, and the bed back to straw. Our windrow sits where the burn pile used to be, which remains the shortest description of what changed on this farm.

The failures, so you can skip them

A compost guide that lists only successes is selling something, so here are the four failures we have personally purchased.

The sour pile. An ammonia or rotten smell means the pile went anaerobic: too wet, packed too tight, or overloaded with greens. The fix is air and carbon, which means turn it, fluff it, and work in dry browns. A pile that smells like a wet swale after a week of rain is the classic October mistake in Laguna, and the cover would have prevented it.

The flies. Fresh manure or kitchen waste left exposed on the surface invites every fly in the barangay. The rule the household guides all repeat: cap every green layer with soil, finished compost, or a thick brown layer, and bury kitchen waste in the pile's core where the heat is. The neighbors' opinion of the farm is a real cost, and the flies are how it gets levied.

The pile that never heats. Cold, quiet, unchanged after three weeks: the pile is too small, too dry, or starving for greens. Rebuild it above the meter mark, water it to the squeeze test, and add manure or a shovel of finished compost. A pile of pure straw is a bale with ambition, not a compost heap, and no activator rescues a pile with nothing for the microbes to eat.

The expectation failure. The most common one, and the least technical: expecting the 2 to 4 week harvest window while running an unactivated, weekly-turned pile, then concluding composting failed when month two arrived. The schedule follows the management, and the guide's fast timeline prices daily turning and an activator, not good intentions. Decide the speed on purpose, then judge the pile by the speed you chose.

Frequently asked questions

How long does composting take in the Philippines?

With an activator and daily turning, a well-built pile is harvestable in 2 to 4 weeks, the schedule the Department of Agriculture's extension guides describe. Slower piles take months: the national engineering standard allows a windrow up to 4 months, and pit methods run about 4 to 5. In-field rice straw sprayed with Trichoderma decomposes in 15 to 20 days. Heat, moisture, and how often the pile is turned decide where a farm lands in that range.

Is it better to burn rice straw or compost it?

Composting keeps the straw's nutrients on the farm, while burning sends them up as smoke, and by IRRI's count 60 to 80 percent of the country's rice straw is burned in the open field. The EcoWaste Coalition puts chemical fertilizer at 800 to 1,000 pesos a bag and counts composted straw against that purchase, and at least one province, Leyte, now bans the open burning outright. Burning is faster for one afternoon and more expensive every season after.

What is the right mix of greens and browns?

The extension guides put the working carbon to nitrogen target at about 30:1, and the national engineering standard accepts anywhere from 25:1 to 40:1. Practically, layer coarse browns such as rice straw, cane tops, and dry leaves against greens such as manure, kitchen waste, and fresh weeds, moistening each layer until a squeezed handful holds together without dripping. Too many browns is slow, too many greens is smelly, and the squeeze test settles both mistakes faster than any formula.

Do I need a compost activator like Trichoderma?

No, but activators buy speed. Compost happens without any help; Trichoderma harzianum or effective microorganisms just shorten the wait, which is why the rapid composting protocols build them in and why a shovel of finished compost from the last pile works nearly as well. A University of the Philippines at Los Banos study in Central Luzon found most rice farmers had never heard of the activator, which is one quiet reason so much straw still burns.
