Wet season planting: when to plant, when to wait

The Philippine rainy season is not a pause in the farming year. It is the farming year. The skill is reading it a week before the forecasts agree, and the signs are on the ground for anyone who knows which ones to watch.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski27 October 2026

Short answerWet season planting in the Philippines is a timing decision, not a date. The weather bureau declares the onset by criteria, but a farm reads the wind switch, the rain that stays overnight, and the ground that stops drying between showers, usually a week before the declaration lands. Rice wants to be planted once the monsoon has settled, with the harvest window checked against the typhoon months before the seed goes in. Most vegetables want the dry season; the wet grows rice, early corn, and the hardy greens. When in doubt, waiting one more settled week costs less than replanting a drowned one.

The wet season is the main event, not the pause

Temperate farming books are written around a growing season squeezed between a frozen winter, and the first thing a Philippine farm has to unlearn is that frame. PAGASA, the national weather bureau, splits our year the other way: a rainy season from June to November and a dry season from December to May, with the dry season itself divided into the cool months from December to February and the hot ones from March to May. Rainfall is not the interruption here. It is the input the whole system runs on, and every decision about seed, labor, and cash is really a decision about where the farm sits inside those six wet months.

The water module makes the same argument from the soil side: in a climate where half the year's water arrives in half the year, the farm's job is to catch the wet season, store it, and spend it slowly. This guide is about the front end of that project, the week the farm decides the rains have truly arrived and puts seed in the ground. Get the week right and the season pays for the whole year. Get it wrong and the season starts with replanting.

One framing before the details. Nobody on this farm has ever heard a neighbor ask when the dry season starts. The question that repeats, at the farm gate and in the waitlist form, is whether the rains have settled enough to plant. That question is what this guide answers, with the government's criteria on one side and the signs our own ground shows on the other.

How the forecast actually decides

The declaration everyone waits for is not a guess. Since its 2019 amendment, PAGASA declares the onset of the rainy season when a five day period, a pentad, in May, June, or July accumulates 25 millimeters of rain or more, with at least 1 millimeter falling on three consecutive days. One wet week somewhere is not enough: at least seven of the thirteen monitoring stations across the country's western, Type I climate section have to meet the rainfall rule at the same time, and in Metro Manila at least two of the three stations must. On top of the rain there is a wind rule, because the rainy season is really a wind season: the prevailing winds must carry a westerly component, from 190 to 280 degrees on the compass, from the surface up to the 850 hectopascal level, which is the atmosphere swapping the northeast easterlies for the southwest monsoon, the habagat. The onset date PAGASA announces is the five day stretch when all of that held together.

The same bureau's climatology puts the normal onset in the second half of May to the first half of June, and its climate monitoring section has described the densest years as landing between about May 27 and June 5. When the declaration comes earlier or later than that, it is news, and the news is worth reading, because the declaration is the signal behind every government planting advisory downstream.

Here is the part the farm has to hold onto, though. The declaration is a national statement built from thirteen stations spread across the western seaboard, and a farm is a microclimate that none of those stations stands in. Calamba sits between the lake and the mountain, Cagayan Valley sits in its own rain shadow arithmetic, and a barangay in Ilocos answers to a different monsoon timetable than a barangay in Bohol. The forecast tells a farm which way the season has tipped. It cannot tell a farm when its own ground is ready. That job still belongs to the signs, which is where the next section lives.

The signs the farm reads a week before the declaration

The bureau watches pentads and wind sectors. We watch four things, and in a normal year they line up about a week ahead of the declaration, which is exactly the head start a farm needs, because land preparation has to start before the planting window, not during it.

  • The wind switch. The habagat announces itself from the southwest, and the first honest sign is direction, not rain. Afternoon clouds start building in the west and leaning over the farm by evening, and the wind that used to arrive off the Pacific arrives smelling of the sea side of the island. On our lot the tell is which end of the farm the afternoon showers come from: easterly storms walk in from the mountain side, monsoon rain walks in from the lake.
  • The rain changes its schedule. April's thunderstorms are theatrical: an hour of drama at four in the afternoon, then stars. Monsoon rain keeps different hours. It arrives late in the day and is still falling after dark, or it settles in as a low grey drizzle that runs all morning. Overnight rain is the signature, because only the monsoon has the moisture supply to rain while the sun is down.
  • The ground stops drying between rains. In a false start, two days of sun crack the soil surface and dust rises off it again. When the season has truly settled, the ground between showers stays dark, the wheelbarrow ruts hold their shape, and the ditches keep standing water overnight. That is the soil telling you the input has arrived in volume, not in visits.
  • The week holds together. The bureau needs three consecutive days with rain; we want the same, because three consecutive days is the difference between weather and a season. Our working rule is the wind switch plus three overnight rains inside a week, with the ground staying dark between them. When all four hold, land preparation starts, whatever the radio says.

The false starts deserve their own warning, because they are the expensive trap in this section. Early May regularly produces genuine thunderstorms, complete with a soaking and a rainbow, and every one of them is a temptation. But those storms ride the easterlies, not the monsoon, and a week of hot sun after them will dry a seedbed into a brick. Plant on a false start and the seed pays for it. Wait for the pattern instead of the puddle.

The planting windows, region by region

The Department of Agriculture maintains agro-climatic advisories that translate the monsoon into planting and harvest windows per region, and the table that circulates most widely is the one the United States Department of Agriculture's Manila office reproduces in its grain reports. For rice, it lists Central Luzon planting from May to June and harvesting September to October; Cagayan Valley planting from June to July, some fields as early as May and as late as August, and harvesting September to October and up to November; Ilocos planting May to June and harvesting August to September, stretching to November; and Western Visayas planting May to June, some fields as early as April, and harvesting September to October. The spread is the point. There is no national planting date; there is a national monsoon arriving on a regional schedule.

A satellite study of the whole rice bowl, built from TerraSAR-X and Sentinel-1 radar imagery over 2016 to 2018, puts hard numbers on the same spread: the wet season planting window runs from April to August with a peak in June and July, and the dry season window runs from September to February with a peak in November and December. The same study found that in most regions the window is more than a hundred days wide. A window that wide is an opportunity and a warning at once, and the warning is the one extension workers repeat every season: plant at the wrong edge of a wide window and the pests and diseases that ride the neighboring plantings arrive with you.

The United Nations' country notes for the Philippines add the north and south split that the Luzon-centered table leaves out: in the north the main rice season is planted June to July and harvested October to December, with a second season planted December to January and harvested April to May; in the south the main season is planted November to January and harvested April to June, with the second planted June to July and harvested October to December. A Mindanao farmer reading a Luzon planting calendar is reading somebody else's monsoon.

For rainfed Ilocos Norte, where the whole game is fitting two crops into one year's rain, a planting calendar study published through CABI runs the arithmetic precisely: sow the rice from the fourth week of May to the second week of June, harvest between the fourth week of September and the second week of October, plant the second crop from the second week of October to the second week of November, and harvest it in January or February. The study's own reason for those dates is the useful part: rainfall tapers off in the last week of September through October, which is exactly what makes harvest weather and drying weather possible. A related weather-based calendar for rainfed lowlands in the same province, worked up at the Mariano Marcos State University, puts the first crop at the third decade of May and the second as early as the third decade of September, with mungbean and other short, drought-tough crops slotted after the second rice on residual moisture. Note what both calendars are really saying: in a rainfed two-crop district, the wet season is so long that its edges, not its middle, are where the profit is decided.

Rice: the two crop arithmetic and the typhoon strait

Now the uncomfortable part of the wet season, the one that shows up in the ledger. The standard Luzon pattern, planted June to July and harvested September to October, puts the harvest exactly where the typhoon record sits. The National Irrigation Administration has put a number on what that overlap costs the country: 500,000 to 600,000 metric tons of palay lost every year to typhoons and floods, mostly in September and October, which is precisely when the wet season crop stands ready to cut. NIA's own response is a calendar adjustment it has been pushing on irrigated systems: plant in October and November instead of June and July, harvest in January and February when the typhoon odds have collapsed, then turn the irrigated fields around fast for a second crop harvested in May and June, before the monsoon returns at full height. The same agency notes the dry season crop generally yields more, because long sunshine hours grow rice better than rain does.

Read against the satellite study, NIA's adjustment is not a radical idea but a shift toward where the dry season window already peaks, November and December. For an irrigated farm the decision is a straight bet: the traditional wet season date buys free water and pays for it with harvest risk; the shifted date buys harvest safety and pays with irrigation dependence and a tighter second crop turnaround. The making it pay module is where that bet gets priced against a specific farm's water bill and land holding.

Rainfed farms cannot shift what they do not control, and the traditional wet season planting stays the main event for them. But the rainfed toolkit has its own hedge, and the FAO country notes name it: sabog tanim, broadcasting seed onto dry soil at the very start of the rains, the establishment method that lets a rainfed farm run rice on rice. The seed sits in the ground and lets the season decide the starting gun, instead of the farmer betting the whole stand on one week's read. Direct seeding trades a crop establishment choice for calendar insurance, and on ground where the onset wobbles, that trade is usually worth it.

One wet season date matters beyond the field edge, and the rice straw biofertilizer guide carries it in detail: straw goes back into a paddy at least 30 days before transplanting, so the decomposers have finished their work before the next crop's roots arrive. That single calendar rule converts the burn decision into a scheduling decision made a month early, and it is the reason our straw sprays go on the week the combine leaves, not the week the seedbed goes in. Field studies in Central Luzon count three to four weeks of land preparation before planting in the wet season, flooding the fields for three to seven days until the soil softens enough to till. The straw rule and the land preparation window are the same window, and the wet season schedule has to hold both.

Vegetables: what the wet season grows and what it kills

Rice gets the calendar attention, but a mixed lot lives or dies on the vegetable side of the wet season, and the honest summary is this: the monsoon grows a short list superbly and murders a longer one slowly. The crops that thrive in our wet are the ones that grew up with it: kangkong that thrives on standing water, alugbati and kamote tops that vine through the rain, okra and squash that shrug off the humidity, and early corn planted into the first settled month. The crops that wait for the dry season are the Mediterranean ones: tomato, eggplant, and pepper, whose flowers drop and whose leaves drown in fungal pressure once the rains settle in. Planting those in July is not farming, it is a lottery ticket with a hoe attached.

The wet season is also the compost season, and the composting guide is the manual. A pile that crawls through April's heat sprints through August's humidity, because the same moisture that drowns a tomato bed powers the decomposers. Every month of monsoon is a month the farm's waste stream turns into next season's fertility for free, and the farm that treats the wet season as lost time is composting with the parking brake on.

The third wet season enterprise is the one the Ilocos calendars already mentioned: the residual moisture crop. After the last rice comes off, the soil holds enough water for one short, tough crop, and mungbean is the classic, maturing on moisture the rice left behind with almost no additional input. On the integrated farming guide's loop map, that mungbean row is a free square on the calendar, and the ducks and the garden beds both eat from it.

When to wait, and what waiting costs

The decision this guide exists for, plant or wait, is a lopsided bet once it is priced. Waiting one more week costs a week of calendar. Planting into an unsettled onset costs the seed twice, the labor of replanting, and two to three weeks of season, and sometimes it costs the stand entirely, because a seedbed that dries out once, floods once, and dries again rarely recovers into an even stand. Run the two prices side by side and the waiting rule writes itself: when the signs disagree, the calendar pays for the doubt, and when they agree, the seed was never at risk.

The exceptions are the reason farming is not arithmetic. A direct seeded paddy converts the question, because broadcast seed on dry ground waits for the rain instead of the farmer waiting for the pattern, which is why sabog tanim survives in exactly the districts where the onset is least reliable. A nursery under cover converts it too: seedlings in seedbed trays ride out a false start for a week and transplant when the signs settle, which is the standard transplanting advantage and the reason we keep the nursery timing flexible rather than fixed. And a late onset does not stretch the harvest end for a rainfed crop planted from residual moisture, which is why the second crop calendars, not the first crop dates, are the ones to guard when the monsoon arrives late.

Our own decision rule is written where the whole crew can see it, because a schedule that lives in one head fails when that head is sick or hauling: wind switched, three overnight rains in the week, ground holding water between showers. All three, go. Two of three, prepare the land and wait. One of three, it is April wearing a costume, and nothing moves.

The failures, so you can skip them

Four ways this season punishes the impatient, all purchased by this farm or its neighbors at full price.

The false start planting. The most common and the most preventable: seed in the ground on the strength of an early May thunderstorm, then a rainless fortnight while the easterlies reassert themselves. The seed either dies in dried crust or emerges into a patchy stand that weeds colonize before the canopy can close. The cure is the four sign rule above, and the discipline to let a good-looking storm pass.

The typhoon strait harvest. The wet season date chosen purely by habit, ignoring that the harvest then stands in September and October exactly where the typhoon record sits. NIA's half million to six hundred thousand tons of annual losses are not bad luck; they are the aggregate of thousands of farms all making the same habit-based bet. The cure is to write the harvest month on the planting decision, before the seed is bought, and to check what the typhoon record says about that month in your own region.

The drowned beds. Tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant forced into the wet season because the seed was on hand and the beds were empty. The plants survive, the harvest never arrives in volume, and the gardener concludes that vegetables are hard in the Philippines rather than that these vegetables belong to a different season. The cure is a wet season crop list taped inside the seed cabinet, and the gardening guide's bed-scale version of the same calendar.

The straw timing miss. Straw incorporated into a paddy two weeks before transplanting, because the schedule behind slipped and the field had to be planted. The decomposers pull nitrogen from the soil exactly where the young crop is root growth, the stand yellows, and the straw gets blamed for a scheduling decision. The cure is the 30 day rule in the biofertilizer guide, and the ferments that let a farm shorten its own decompose time when the calendar slips anyway.

If you take one thing from this page, take the lopsided bet: waiting a settled week costs days, planting an unsettled one costs seasons. The forecast narrows the odds, the signs call the week, and the two together are the whole skill.

Frequently asked questions

When does the rainy season start in the Philippines?

PAGASA declares the onset when a five day period between May and July totals 25 millimeters of rain or more with at least 1 millimeter on three consecutive days, confirmed at seven or more of its thirteen western monitoring stations, with the winds carrying a westerly component. The normal onset is the second half of May to the first half of June, most years between about May 27 and June 5, and the rainy season then runs to November.

When should rice be planted in the wet season?

The Department of Agriculture's regional advisories put the wet season planting window at May to June for Central Luzon, Ilocos, and Western Visayas, and June to July for Cagayan Valley, with a satellite study finding the national window spans April to August and peaks in June and July. The decisive check is on the harvest side: a June to July planting harvests in September and October, where the typhoon record sits, which is why irrigated farms are being urged toward October and November plantings harvested in January and February.

What can I plant during the rainy season besides rice?

The wet season grows kangkong, alugbati, kamote tops, okra, squash, and early corn, and it is the best season of the year for compost piles and liquid ferments. Tomatoes, eggplant, and peppers wait for the dry season, because rain humidity drops their flowers and invites fungal disease. After the rice harvest, mungbean and other short, drought-tough crops can grow on the residual moisture with almost no extra input.

Is it safe to plant at the very start of the rains?

Not on the strength of one storm. Early May thunderstorms ride the easterlies and are routinely followed by weeks of hot sun that kill a seedbed, so the farm waits for the settled pattern: the wind switched to the southwest, three overnight rains inside a week, and ground that stays dark between showers. Direct seeding onto dry ground is the hedge where the onset is unreliable, because the seed waits in the soil for the season instead of the farmer betting the stand on one week's read.
