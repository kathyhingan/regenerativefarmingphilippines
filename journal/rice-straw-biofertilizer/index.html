Rice straw biofertilizer: the input your farm already grows

The straw does not have to wait for the pile. Fermented from the farm's own plants and microbes, the liquid ferments are the fastest route from waste to fertility, and the equipment list is shorter than you fear.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski26 October 2026

Short answerRice straw biofertilizer is the liquid half of the waste answer: ferments made from the farm's own plants, microbes, and sugar, sprayed back onto the straw windrow, the beds, and the paddy. Here are the three ferments a small farm actually runs, the short equipment list, and what the practice does and does not replace.

Why the straw bill keeps coming back

Every guide in this journal keeps meeting the same villain, and the composting guide already introduced him properly: the match. IRRI's RiceStrawPH project counts the country's rice crop at 15.2 million tons a year, which leaves about 11.3 million tons of straw spread across the fields, and counts 60 to 80 percent of it burned in the open field. The windrow is the slow answer to that smoke, the one that turns straw, manure, and crop waste into the farm's cheapest fertilizer over weeks and months.

This guide is the fast answer. A pile takes a season; a ferment takes a week. The same biology that slowly composts straw in a windrow can be cultured in a bucket, multiplied with a sack of sugar, and sprayed back onto the straw, the beds, and the paddy while the pile is still heating up. Farmers have run versions of this for as long as there have been farms, and the government's extension system now documents the recipes carefully enough that a family can follow them with kitchen equipment. What the documents still skip, as always, is the part where the weather, the labor, and the calendar argue back. That part is below, in days and pesos.

One thing the ferments are not, so we can put it down early: they are not a substitute for the pile. The straw's bulk and carbon still belong in compost and bedding, and the ferments work alongside it, seeding the pile with the same microbes they carry. Readers who want the full weight of the slow route should read the composting guide first and come back here for the liquid half.

What biofertilizer actually is, and what it is not

The word travels with a laboratory accent, but the thing itself is plain. Biofertilizers are products that contain living microorganisms which help supply nutrients to the crop and improve the soil while they are at it: bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air, microbes that unlock phosphorus and potassium locked in the soil, fungi that extend a root's reach. The Department of Agriculture's regional offices describe them exactly that way when they promote the registered products, and their pitch to farmers is the one that matters to this guide's arithmetic: biofertilizers cost less than pure chemical fertilizer, and they can cut chemical fertilizer use by 30 to 50 percent.

Two things follow from the definition, and both protect a beginner from disappointment. First, a biofertilizer is alive, which means it can be killed: by chlorine in the water, by the midday sun in the sprayer, by a sealed bottle that runs out of air. Second, it is not fertilizer in the bag-of-nitrogen sense. It is a workforce, not a delivery. The cells in a dose of biofertilizer carry a few grams of nutrients at most; their value is the months of nutrient cycling they start, which is why the honest sources, PhilRice included, position them inside a balanced fertilization program rather than as a replacement for it.

The government regulates the commercial side for exactly this reason. The Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority keeps a registry of biofertilizer products, and the Department of Agriculture's own news service prices the commercially produced ones at 500 to 5,000 pesos per hectare, depending on the ingredients and the technology behind them. That range is worth staring at, because it contains the whole case for this guide: the low end of that range is the price of sugar, and the farm already owns the rest of the ingredients.

The three ferments a small farm actually runs

The Department of Agriculture's extension arm publishes a bio-organic inputs guide that reads like a menu of ferments, and most small farms settle on three of them. They share one logic: culture the microorganisms that do the work, multiply them with sugar, and apply the result where it is needed.

Fermented plant juice, or FPJ. Young plant material chopped and pressed with molasses or muscovado sugar until the microbes in the leaves turn the sugar into a brown, sweet-smelling liquid concentrate. It is the growth tonic of the set: rich in the plant's own juices and enzymes, applied as a foliar spray or a soil drench at a couple of tablespoons per liter of water. It costs whatever the sugar costs, because the plants are weeds and tops the farm was going to throw away.

Indigenous microorganisms, or IMO. A cultured collection of the fungi and bacteria that already decompose things on the farm's own ground. Cooked rice is left out where decomposition is already happening, the molds arrive on their own, and the catch is cultured further with sugar water until it becomes a liquid concentrate or a solid carrier. IMO is the decomposer of the set, and it is the one pointed directly at the straw windrow.

The effective microorganism route, or EMAS and EMBI. Where a farm buys a commercial culture instead of catching its own, the effective microorganism solutions are the common product, and PhilRice's organic nutrient management guide documents how they anchor a rice straw inoculant: mixed with rice bran and carbonized rice hull into a solid carrier that is broadcast onto the paddy to hasten straw decomposition. This is the version that connects the ferments to the biggest straw problem of all, the one sitting in the paddy.

Every recipe below is written to be run in one afternoon of labor and checked in one minute a day. The farm runs all three, in the order they are written, and the sections after the recipes carry the failures so the first batch is not the tuition.

Fermented plant juice: the seven day recipe

FPJ is the right first ferment because it is almost impossible to get wrong and it teaches every habit the other two require. The extension guide's proportions are three parts chopped plant material to one part molasses by weight; the household versions that circulate in farming groups run two kilos of kangkong or kamote tops to one kilo of brown sugar, which lands in the same neighborhood. The steps:

  • Collect before sunrise. The plants are the crop: kangkong, kamote tops, banana stalks, alugbati, young weeds pulled before they seed, the growing tips of anything vigorous. The guides say harvest early in the morning while the plants are still full of the night's moisture and the microbes are on the leaf surfaces. Do not wash the material; the dust and the leaf surfaces are where the microbes live, and water is for later.
  • Chop fine and mix by hand. Cut everything into pieces about an inch across, put it in a basin with the sugar or molasses, and mix with bare hands until every piece is coated. The sugar pulls the juice out of the plant by osmosis, which is the whole engine of the ferment.
  • Pack and cover. Put the mixture in a pail or clay jar, pressed down gently, and cover it with unprinted manila paper tied with string. The cover keeps flies and rain out while letting fermentation gases escape; a screw lid left loose does the same. Label it with the contents, the start date, and the expected harvest date, because in seven days nobody remembers.
  • Wait seven days. Store the container in a cool, shady place. Around day seven the liquid that has gathered is brown to yellow-brown and smells sweet and faintly alcoholic. That smell is the pass mark. A rotten smell means the batch failed, which the failures section explains.
  • Press and bottle. Squeeze the spent plant material hard to extract the last of the juice, strain the liquid through cloth or a fine net, and bottle it in dark glass or plastic. Store the bottles in a cool dark place and do not tighten the caps all the way: the culture keeps breathing for months, and a sealed bottle is how a good ferment becomes a small explosion on a shelf. The guides give FPJ a shelf life of up to six months, and our oldest usable bottle has been older than that.
  • Use at two tablespoons per liter. That is the extension guide's working dilution, sprayed on leaves or drenched on soil around the base of plants, weekly or by the vigor of the crop. Morning or late afternoon is the application window, the same one chemical sprays prefer, because the sun is not boiling the spray dry.

The spent, pressed plant material is not waste: it goes straight into the compost pile, where it arrives pre-inoculated and sweet. The ferment's byproduct feeds the pile that the ferment's product will eventually spray, which is the sort of loop this farm's whole method is built on.

Indigenous microorganisms: farming the microbes first

FPJ cultures the juice; IMO cultures the workforce. The recipe in the extension guide is almost embarrassingly simple, and its logic is worth stating because it is the foundation of the whole practice: the microorganisms that know how to decompose a farm's waste already live on that farm, and they can be invited rather than bought.

  • Set the table. Cook a pot of rice, let it cool, and pack it into a bamboo trough or a wooden box. Cover it with a fine screen against rats and tie it down.
  • Park it where decomposition already happens. The guide says shaded ground near decaying crops: under the banana clump, beside the compost pile, at the edge of the bamboo, in the leaf litter under the hedge. On our lot the best collection spot has always been under the mango tree where the bedding sweepings rot, and the molds arrive like they were waiting.
  • Collect after five to seven days. White mold threads across the rice are the catch. What to avoid is just as clear: black and green molds mean the wrong crowd arrived, and the batch goes to the compost, not the bucket.
  • Culture it. Mix the molded rice with an equal weight of muscovado sugar and a liter of clean, non-chlorinated water for every kilo of rice, into a pail filled only three quarters full, covered with manila paper. After seven days in a cool spot, strain the liquid. That extract is the IMO concentrate, stored like FPJ in dark bottles with loose caps.
  • Use at two tablespoons per liter for routine spraying, exactly like FPJ. The extension guide's rice field directions are more generous: spread freshly threshed straw across the paddy and spray it at eight tablespoons per liter, twice, before land preparation.

That last line is the bridge from this recipe to the guide's title, and it is the single highest-value trick in the whole set for a rice farm: the straw does not go to the microbes, the microbes go to the straw, at rates the government's own guide publishes. The next section puts numbers on that.

The bran carrier: EMBI and bokashi for the paddy

Liquid ferments are awkward to broadcast across a hectare, so the extension system solidifies them onto carriers. PhilRice's organic nutrient management guide documents the rice straw inoculant, EMBI, as a fermented mixture of one part darak, which is rice bran, one part carbonized rice hull, five percent effective microorganism activated solution, and five percent molasses. The activated solution itself is a five to seven day ferment of the commercial culture with molasses and water, so a farm already running FPJ and IMO has every skill this needs except buying the starter culture once.

The carrier is broadcast onto the paddy with the straw or other organic material, and the timing rule that comes with it is the one to respect: straw should go back into the field at least 30 days before transplanting, three to four weeks of decomposition before the next crop's roots arrive. That calendar rule is what makes the inoculant strategic rather than decorative, because it converts the burn decision into a scheduling decision made a month ahead of land preparation.

The same guide and the bio-organic inputs menu carry bokashi, the solid fermentation bed, for farms that want volume. The working mix is one part IMO concentrate to four parts water, added to rice bran until the material holds together in a squeezed fist without dripping, layered with chicken dung between beds of chopped straw about two meters by six, covered with plastic, and turned after a week. Bokashi is composting with the inoculant built in from the first layer, and it finishes in weeks rather than months. It is the middle option between the windrow and the spray bottle, and for a mixed lot with more manure than spray bottles can visit, it is the one we run most often.

Pointing the ferments at the straw

Each ferment has a natural target, and the straw is connected to all of them:

  • The windrow. Every new layer of the compost pile gets a watering of FPJ or IMO at two tablespoons per liter. The composting guide describes the activator's role in buying weeks; a home-fermented culture does the same job for the price of sugar, and a shovel of finished compost does it for free.
  • The paddy. IMO at eight tablespoons per liter, sprayed on the fresh straw twice before land preparation, or the EMBI bran carrier broadcast with the straw, at least 30 days before transplanting.
  • The growing crop. This is where the government's application schedule is worth printing and following. A Department of Agriculture administrative order on biofertilizer use for rice lays out four applications: at seedbed stage 10 to 15 days after sowing, then 10 to 15, 20 to 25, and 30 to 35 days after transplanting, at one liter per hectare per spray. The same order carries the handling rules that keep a culture alive: never mix it with chlorinated water, never mix it with pesticides, and apply it a day before or a day after any inorganic fertilizer.

Our own practice compresses that schedule into a habit: the ferment sprayer rides on the same walks that water and inspect the beds, and the paddy gets its two straw sprays in the week the combine leaves. A schedule that depends on memory fails; a schedule that rides on work that was happening anyway survives a wet season.

The equipment list, itemized

The queue entry that promised this guide said the equipment list is shorter than you fear, so here it is in full, for all three ferments: a bolo and a chopping surface, a basin, two pails, a bamboo trough or a wooden box for the IMO stage, unprinted manila paper, string, dark bottles, and a sack of muscovado sugar or molasses. A kitchen strainer and a funnel finish the set. Nothing on the list needs to be bought new except possibly the sugar, and the bottles are last month's juice and vinegar.

The honest additions are not equipment. Labels and a notebook matter more than any of it, because a farm running three ferments at once is running a small brewery with no brewmaster, and the difference between a shelf of working cultures and a shelf of mystery liquids is whether anyone wrote the dates down. The other addition is a dedicated sprayer: the administrative order's rule against mixing biofertilizers with pesticide residue is a rule about equipment as much as chemistry, and a sprayer that has carried herbicide once can carry that memory for a long time.

What it costs, and what it replaces

Start with the commercial anchor. The Department of Agriculture's news service puts registered biofertilizer products at 500 to 5,000 pesos per hectare, and the department's regional guidance credits the practice with a 30 to 50 percent reduction in chemical fertilizer use. Set those two numbers against the fertilizer bill the farm already pays, which the making it pay module tracks line by line: the EcoWaste Coalition's statement during the straw burning debates priced the standard chemical fertilizer at 800 to 1,000 pesos a bag, and a hectare of rice is not fed by one bag.

The fermented, on-farm version's cost is sugar and attention. A batch of FPJ uses a kilo of molasses or muscovado against plant material the farm was discarding; an IMO batch uses the same sugar against cooked rice and a week of patience. Even doubling and tripling the batches for a hectare's worth of spray leaves the input bill an order of magnitude below the low end of the commercial range. What the farm buys with that saving is not zero fertilizer but a slower climb: PhilRice's balanced fertilization work is the reference here, reducing inorganic input without hurting yield by combining it with organic materials and biofertilizers, applied to a soil test rather than to habit.

So the honest ledger reads: the ferments trim the fertilizer purchase line season by season, they delete the activator purchase from the compost operation entirely, and they convert the straw from a disposal problem into a feedstock. What they do not do is replace the soil test, the compost pile, or the first season's expectations. The compost and biofertilizers module carries the full peso arithmetic of the fertility loop.

The failures, so you can skip them

A guide that lists only successes is selling something, so here are the four failures we have personally purchased, each one priced in sugar.

The chlorinated batch. Tap water in much of the Philippines is chlorinated, and chlorine kills the cultures this whole guide is about. The administrative order says it plainly for the commercial products, and it is just as true for a homemade ferment: mix and dilute with rain water, well water, or water left standing uncovered overnight. This failure is silent, which is what makes it expensive: the batch smells fine and does nothing.

The cooked culture. Every ferment in this guide is a living thing parked in a bottle, and the two common ways to kill it are the noon sun on a windowsill and a tight cap. Store dark, store cool, cap loose, and remember the bottles breathe: the guides' six month shelf life assumes it. A ferment that smells like vinegar's angry cousin has usually been cooked or starved of air.

The wrong plant material. FPJ wants young, juicy, fast-growing tissue, harvested before sunrise and never washed. Woody stems give little juice, washed leaves have lost their microbial passengers, and material collected hours after cutting has dried on the way to the pail. The other rule is situational: material from plants recently sprayed with anything is material inoculating your ferment with whatever was sprayed.

The calendar failure. The quiet one, and the most expensive: treating a week-long ferment like a same-day input. The paddy rule is the example that costs the most, because straw incorporated less than 30 days before transplanting can tie up nitrogen exactly when the crop needs it, which is how a good practice gets blamed for a bad schedule. Write the ferment dates on the same calendar as the planting dates, or the ferments will arrive a week after the field needed them.

How the ferments fit the method

The ferments are one instrument in the method's fertility section, and the rest of the site carries the system around them. The compost and biofertilizers module is the textbook version of everything this guide ran on the ground. The soil module follows the thread the ferments pull: feed the biology and the biology feeds the plant. The integrated farming guide maps the loop the ferments accelerate, where each enterprise's waste becomes the next one's input, and the gardening guide runs the whole practice at kitchen scale for readers starting with a bed and a pail rather than a hectare.

If you take one thing from this page, take the bridge line: the straw does not go to the microbes, the microbes go to the straw. The farm's biggest waste stream and its cheapest input are the same pile of straw, and the difference between them is a bucket, a sack of sugar, and a week.

Frequently asked questions

What is rice straw biofertilizer?

It is any living microbe fertilizer the farm ferments from its own waste, with rice straw playing both roles: feedstock and target. Fermented plant juice, indigenous microorganisms, and effective microorganism solutions are the common ones, and all of them are sprayed onto straw to speed its breakdown or onto crops to feed the soil's biology. The Department of Agriculture describes the commercial versions the same way: substances containing living microorganisms that help supply nutrients and improve soil health.

How much does biofertilizer cost a small farm?

The Department of Agriculture's news service prices commercially produced biofertilizer at 500 to 5,000 pesos per hectare, depending on the ingredients and the technology behind it. The fermented version a farm makes itself costs a kilo or two of muscovado sugar or molasses per batch, clean water, and the labor of chopping and checking, because the microbes and the plant material are farm waste. The equipment is already in the farm kitchen.

Does biofertilizer replace chemical fertilizer?

It trims the bill rather than deleting it, and the honest sources say so. Department of Agriculture regional guidance credits good biofertilizer use with a 30 to 50 percent reduction in chemical fertilizer, and PhilRice's balanced fertilization work reduces inorganic input without hurting yield by combining it with organic materials and biofertilizers against a soil test. Treat the ferments as a supplement that shrinks the purchase line season by season, not a full replacement in year one.

How long does it take to make biofertilizer from farm waste?

Fermented plant juice finishes in about seven days, effective microorganism solutions ferment in five to seven, and indigenous microorganisms take five to seven days to collect plus another seven to culture. Everything keeps for months in dark bottles with loose caps. Set against a compost windrow's weeks or months, the liquid ferments are the fast lane of the same waste to fertility project.
