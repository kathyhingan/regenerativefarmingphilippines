Integrated farming in the Philippines: one land, many harvests

Crops, animals, and fish on the same lot, each one paying part of the other's bills. This is the loop map we run, and the order to build it in.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski24 October 2026

Short answerIntegrated farming closes loops between crops, livestock, and fish so one enterprise's waste becomes the next one's input. On a small Philippine farm that means rice straw feeds the animals, manure feeds the beds and the pond, and three income lines replace one. Here is the loop map, the starter sequence, and the honest costs.

What an integrated farming system is

An integrated farming system is one piece of land carrying two or more farm enterprises that trade inputs with each other. The crops feed the animals, the animals feed the soil and the pond, the pond waters the crops, and around it goes. The word to hold onto is enterprise, not project: a bed of pechay is a crop, but a standing population of chickens that turns kitchen and garden waste into eggs and manure is an enterprise, and integration happens between enterprises.

The idea is old in this country, older than the term. A rice paddy with a few ducks patrolling it, a pig pen at the back of the vegetable lot, a fish pond dug in the lowest corner where the water already collects: plenty of grandparents ran versions of this without ever calling it a system. What the modern version adds is intent. Instead of the animals happening to be there, the farm designs the flows on purpose, so that each enterprise's waste stream is the next enterprise's feedstock, and nothing that leaves the gate is bought twice.

The country's own rice researchers have pushed diversified rice-based farming for years under the name Palayamanan, and extension offices teach versions of it as integrated farming systems. Government PDFs and seminars describe it well. What they rarely show is the sequence: which loop to close first, which one to leave for year two, and where the whole plan usually goes wrong. That sequence is what this guide is for, and it comes from running the loops on our own lot in Calamba rather than from a slide deck.

One definition note before the practice, because the terms get mixed. Integrated farming is about connections between enterprises. Mixed cropping or polyculture is about several crops sharing one field. Crop rotation is about time, one crop following another through the seasons. All three belong to the same family of ideas, and a regenerative farm usually runs all three, but this guide is about the first: the animal, crop, and fish enterprises linked by their waste streams. The definition page settles the bigger vocabulary questions around the word regenerative.

The loop map: waste becomes feed becomes fertility

The cleanest way to understand an integrated farm is to follow the straw. On a typical rice farm, the straw is a problem to be burned, and the smoke that hangs over Laguna after harvest is the visible cost of that decision. On an integrated farm the straw is a raw material. Composted, it becomes bed fertility. Fed dry as roughage, it carries large animals through the weeks when grass is short. Spread deep as bedding in a pen, it soaks up manure all season and comes out the far end as finished compost worth carrying to any bed on the farm.

Follow the manure next, because that is where the loop turns the corner. Composted with the straw, it feeds the vegetable beds. Steeped and diluted as a liquid feed, it waters the same beds at the same time. Washed into a pond, it feeds the algae, the algae feed the tilapia, and the pond bottom, rich and dark after a season, is lifted out and carried to the beds as pond muck, which is compost by another name. Our compost module carries the full recipes in days and pesos, because the loop only pays if the fermentation is done properly.

Then the pond water itself. The nutrient-loaded water that would foul a pond is exactly what a vegetable bed wants, so the pump schedule that freshens the pond doubles as irrigation that fertilizes. And the animals close the last loop in the quietest way: ducks sent through the paddies and beds after harvest eat the snails and fallen grain that would otherwise be next season's pests, which is pest control delivered on webbed feet. The pests module explains why that patrol works and where it stops working.

Read the map from above and the pattern is one input, many exits. Sun and rain grow the crops; the crops' residues feed the animals; the animals' wastes feed the soil, the pond, and the next crop; the pond's water and muck feed everything downstream. The farm's only real imports are the things it cannot make, which on a well-run lot shrink to stockfeed concentrate, seed, and the occasional medicine. Every loop that closes is a line that disappears from the purchase list, which is the same test our comparison guide uses to tell regenerative from merely sustainable.

Why small Philippine farms win at integration

There is a reason integration suits this country's farms, and it is not nostalgia. Most Philippine farms are small, and smallness is an advantage in exactly the ways integration rewards. Distances are short, so a wheelbarrow of manure reaches its destination before it is a chore. Boundaries are tight, so one family can watch chickens, pigs, fish, and beds in the same hour. And the household eats from the farm, so multiple small harvests, eggs this week, fish this month, vegetables continuously, convert directly into grocery money that stays in the pocket instead of passing through a market stall.

The deeper reason is risk. A specialized farm owns one income line, and one income line owns the family. A typhoon, a price collapse, or a disease outbreak in the single crop lands on the household with nothing to break its fall. An integrated farm holds three or four lines, and they do not all fail in the same weather: a storm that flattens the rice leaves the pond full and the pens standing, a low price for pork leaves the eggs and vegetables paying the week's bills. Our making it pay module is built on this arithmetic, because resilience on a family farm is not an abstraction. It is the difference between a bad season and a lost farm.

Integration also solves the labor calendar's cruelest problem, which is the gap between harvests. Rice pays twice a year, sugarcane once, and between those dates a specialized farm has income and then a long silence. An integrated farm fills the silences with the enterprises whose clock runs faster: eggs daily, vegetables in weeks, fish in months. The money arrives in smaller amounts but continuously, and continuous beats large in a household budget, as any farm wife managing between harvests will tell you.

There is a land-use argument too, and it matters most exactly where land is tightest. The farm's margins, wet corners, and shaded strips, the parts too small or too awkward for a crop row, are precisely where integration puts its enterprises: a pen on the high corner, a pond in the wet depression that never grew a decent stand of anything, forage along the fence lines. The lot's worst ground becomes its most productive ground, which is the quiet way integrated farming raises a farm's output without buying a single extra square meter.

The loops we run on our own ground

Our lot in Calamba carries dragonfruit, rice, sugarcane, vegetables, and livestock, three generations on the same land. The loop map above is not theory here; it is the layout of the place, and each loop earned its place by replacing a purchase. The rice straw goes two ways: composted for the vegetable beds, and bedding for the pens, where it turns into the darkest compost on the farm by the time the pens are mucked. The sugarcane fields' tops and the vegetable lots' waste go to the animals. The animals' manure, composted, returns to every bed on the lot, and the compost windrow sits where the old burn pile used to be, which is as good a one-line history of this farm as any.

The pond earns its corner twice. Dug in the low ground where water already stood after every heavy rain, it takes the runoff that used to drown a corner of the vegetable lot, raises tilapia in it, and sends its nutrient-rich water to the beds on pump days. After a season the pond muck comes out to the beds, and the loop that started as a drainage problem ends as fish harvests and fertilizer. The water module tells the drainage half of that story, because in a two-season country the water schedule is the farm's real clock.

The poultry patrol is the loop visitors notice last and ask about first. Ducks move through the paddies and beds after harvest, cleaning up fallen grain and snails, and their manure lands where the next crop needs it. It is not a complete pest program and we never present it as one; it is one layer among the habitat and hand-picking layers the pests module describes. But it is the layer that pays for its own feed, which is the standard every loop on this farm has to meet.

What we run it for is the purchase list. Every loop closed removed a line from it, and the lines that remain, concentrate feed, seed we do not yet save, the animals' medicines, are the honest edges of the system. That shrinking list is also the measure we report in the measuring module, because a farm's direction is visible in its receipts long before it is visible in the soil, and receipts do not flatter.

The starter sequence: one loop at a time

The mistake almost everyone makes, and we made it too, is starting with the animals. Livestock are the most appealing enterprise and the least forgiving first one: they eat every day whether or not the farm's feed loops exist yet, and an integrated system that begins with purchased feed has built a cost center, not a loop. The order that works is waste first, animals second, water last, and each step funds the next.

Start with compost, because every farm already produces its own feedstock and the compost module needs nothing but the straw, the manure from whatever animals are already there, and days of fermentation. Compost is the loop that pays from the first batch: it replaces fertilizer purchases immediately, and it teaches the discipline the rest of the system runs on, which is handling other enterprises' waste before it becomes a problem rather than after.

Second, add the smallest animal enterprise the household can watch daily, which for most families is poultry. Birds turn kitchen waste, garden waste, and purchased concentrate into eggs and manure at a scale a child can help manage, and the manure is the feedstock that makes the compost loop stronger. Keep it small enough that its feed comes mostly from the farm's own waste streams, because the moment concentrate dominates, the loop is running backward and the purchase list is growing again.

Third, and only if the land offers the low corner for it, dig the pond. Water is the loop with the highest setup cost and the longest memory: a pond placed where water already collects pays for itself in drainage, fish, and irrigation from day one, and a pond placed where the farmer wishes water collected pays for a pump bill forever. Our first season module carries the sequencing logic for a first year, and the same one-thing-at-a-time rule holds: the pond joins a working system, it does not anchor one.

The last step is not a step at all but a habit: write the flows down. Which enterprise's waste went where, in what quantity, in which week. The notebook is what turns a collection of enterprises into a system, because the loops you can see are the loops you can tighten, and the ones you cannot see quietly become purchases again. The beginner path on this site runs the same rule at every scale: one loop, written down, beats five loops run from memory.

What integration does not fix

An honest guide has to say where the model strains, because integration is not a remedy for every farm problem and pretending otherwise is how good systems get abandoned. The first strain is skill. Each enterprise is its own craft, and a family that must learn poultry, pond, and plant health in the same year will learn some of them from expensive mistakes. The second is labor peaks: the loops share waste, but they do not share the moments that all demand attention at once, a pen to muck, a pond to refresh, a bed to plant, and a typhoon two days out. Third, feed: until the waste loops mature, the animals eat purchases, and the purchase list grows before it shrinks.

Disease is the fourth strain and the one extension leaflets mention least. Animals sharing ground with crops share their problems too; a parasite load that recycles happily between pen and pasture can build faster than it would in a specialized setup, and integration done without rotation and rest becomes a disease conveyor. The countermeasures are unglamorous: paddock moves, dry periods for runs, clean water, and the willingness to cull rather than medicate a lost cause. The stewardship page covers the standards side of this, because a farm that integrates well is a farm that watches closely.

And integration does not forgive scale done backward. The model works because each enterprise's waste is the next one's input at roughly matching volumes: a handful of pigs produce more manure than a small vegetable lot can absorb, and a pond sized beyond the manure that feeds it stays clear and hungry. Every loop must be sized to the loop beside it, which is why we say start small and add slowly. An oversized loop is just a second specialized farm on the same lot, wearing integration as a costume.

Is integrated farming the same as regenerative farming

No, and the difference matters to how you run both. Integration describes connections: waste streams trading between enterprises on one piece of land. Regeneration describes direction: the ground, the biology, and the purchase list measurably improving season over season. An integrated farm can absolutely be degenerating if its loops are run hard, its soil left bare, its pond overloaded, its animals stocked past what the land recycles. The loops move nutrients around; they do not by themselves make the ground better.

Run in the regenerative direction, though, integration is the strongest delivery system the method has. The loops are how a farm stops buying fertility and starts making it, how soil cover and living roots persist through the seasons, how the purchase list shrinks line by line. The ten modules teach the practices, and integration is the farm layout that lets each practice feed the next. That is the honest relationship: integration is the plumbing, regeneration is the direction the water flows, and a farm needs both before the word system earns its place on the sign.

Frequently asked questions

What is an integrated farming system?

It is one piece of land carrying two or more farm enterprises that trade inputs with each other: crops feed animals, animal manure feeds the soil and the fish pond, pond water and muck feed the crops. The defining feature is the designed connections between enterprises, so each one's waste becomes the next one's input and the farm buys less from outside.

Is integrated farming the same as regenerative farming?

No. Integration describes connections between enterprises; regenerative describes the direction the ground is moving, with soil, biology, and the purchase list improving season over season. An integrated farm can still be degenerating if its loops are run hard and its soil left bare. Run in the regenerative direction, integration is the layout that makes each practice feed the next.

Which enterprise should a small farm start with?

Start with compost, because the feedstock is already on the farm and the first batch replaces fertilizer purchases immediately. Add poultry second, small enough to live on the farm's own waste streams, and dig a pond third, only in the low corner where water already collects. Animals first is the classic beginner's mistake: they eat every day whether or not the feed loops exist yet.

How much land does integrated farming need?

Less than most people assume, because integration runs on margins and corners rather than extra area: a pen on the high side, a pond in the wet depression, forage along the fences. What it needs is matching volumes between neighboring loops, not a big lot. A small farm with tight loops beats a large one with oversized, half-connected enterprises every season.
