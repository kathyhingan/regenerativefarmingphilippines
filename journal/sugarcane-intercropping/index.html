Sugarcane intercropping Philippines: farm the empty rows

The most expensive ground on a cane farm is the ground between the rows, and for the first third of the cycle it grows nothing but weeds. This guide is about renting it out to something short, quick, and useful.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski29 October 2026

Short answerA cane field spends its first three or four months as mostly empty space, and the rows between cane are wide enough to farm. Short rice, maize, mungbean, or vegetables in the free interrows earn the block a second harvest the cane never asks for, and Philippine trials put the bonus anywhere from a few sacks of rice to tens of thousands of pesos a hectare.

The empty months in a cane field

Walk a newly planted cane block in its first month and read what you see: long straight furrows of waiting ground. Sugarcane is the widest-rowed crop on most Philippine farms, planted in rows from a meter to a meter and a half apart or wider, and it is in no hurry to fill them. The cane setts take two to three weeks to germinate, the stool grows slowly for months, and on plant cane the leaves do not close over the rows until four or five months after planting. On ratoon, the regrowth from the old stool closes canopy earlier, around three to three and a half months. Those figures come from the Mauritius Sugar Industry Research Institute's long intercropping program, carried in a resource collection we return to often, and they describe the plant's growth habit rather than a place, which is why they travel.

During exactly those months the farm is spending, not earning. Land preparation, seed pieces, the first feed, the weeding passes, all of it goes out against a harvest that arrives only after the better part of a year, when the mill's schedule and the district's cutting order finally call the block. A cane farmer's cash flow is a single deep breath held for eleven or twelve months. The intercrop is the exhale in the middle: a crop fitted into the space and the season the cane is not using, harvested and sold or eaten long before the cane canopy shuts the door. Researchers in Mauritius put it in one line we have never bettered: intercropping on land previously used solely for cane is almost the same as finding new land.

On our own blocks in Calamba the practice is older than the word regenerative. Willy's father planted peanuts between the cane rows when the price of sugar went soft, and the family habit since has been to put something short in every other interrow, every season, without asking permission from anyone. The question that turned this guide from habit into writing came at a barangay meeting, from a younger farmer who had just taken over his family's cane land in Batangas: what exactly can you put between the rows, what does the cane forgive, and what does the first year actually pay. The research below answers him better than the family folklore did.

What the Philippine research actually says

The strongest recent evidence is a two-year trial by the National Research Council of the Philippines, a DOST council, published as the study behind its rice-sugarcane intercropping e-Gallery entry. The design is a catalog of the choices a real farmer faces: two upland rice varieties, NSIC Rc 27 and PSB Rc 10, two furrow spacings for the rice, 1.0 and 1.5 meters, and two seeding rates, 40 and 80 kilograms per hectare, in a proper replicated trial across the 2023 and 2024 seasons. The result that matters is boring in the best way: the intercropping system consistently beat sugarcane monocropping on every productivity measure the trial used, the land equivalent ratio, the net effect ratio, and the transgressive overyielding index, in every season. The best combination, rice at 1.0 meter spacing with NSIC Rc 27 at the 80 kilogram rate, produced the highest combined score. On the money side, the economic analysis put the extra income from a suitable intercrop at nearly 20,000 pesos per hectare, with sugarcane still the primary contributor and the rice a secondary but real second income off the same ground.

The government of Negros Occidental has run the same play at provincial scale, and its numbers come from a different direction. The province's Sugarcane-Rice Cropping System program, run with the Sugar Regulatory Administration, the Department of Agriculture, and the Office of the Provincial Agriculturist, leans on a Philippine Sugar Research Institute study conducted in Victorias City three decades ago: rice intercropped into cane produced 23 cavans of palay, twelve sacks of rice, per hectare of sugarcane. The provincial agriculturist, Japhet Masculino, framed the motive in an interview SunStar carried: the province catches rice shortage in the lean months from June to August, the tiempos muertos, and its cane land is enormous, around 190,000 hectares. His arithmetic is worth repeating because it shows the ceiling: at ten sacks of palay per hectare across the province's cane land, the intercrop alone would mean 1.9 million bags of palay. The province seeded the program with drought-resistant, short-duration rice from the DA, 90 to 95 days to harvest, put pilot sites in Hinigaran and block farms across six more cities and towns, and the governor allocated 10 million pesos for seed, inputs, training, and mechanization. At the time of that report, around 20 hectares were already intercropped and small farmers were signing on after the milling season.

And the third source is a state university doing it in public. The Central Philippines State University in Kabankalan City launched a sugarcane-upland rice project in August 2020 on five hectares of its reservation, intercropping traditional upland varieties such as Kalubid and Malagaya into the cane, with a four-hectare techno-demo alongside it planted to NSIC 27 in partnership with the Office of the Provincial Agriculturist and the SRA. Three independent kinds of institution, a DOST research council, a provincial government, and a university, all pointing at the same practice with numbers attached, is as close to settled as farm questions get. Nobody in that list describes rice between cane as experimental. The risk left is execution, which is what the rest of this guide is for.

Choosing the intercrop: four rules and a shortlist

The Mauritius program spent decades learning what succeeds between cane rows, and its findings compress into four rules that every successful intercrop we have seen or run obeys. They are worth memorizing as a set, because each one exists to protect the cane, which remains the farm's paycheck.

  • Short cycle. The intercrop must finish inside the cane's open window, which means 85 to 95 days for the longest cereals and less for almost everything else. A crop that is still standing when the canopy closes is a crop now stealing light from the cane.
  • Short stature. Anything that grows past about two meters shades the cane rows beside it. Tall maize varieties and climbing vines on tall trellises are the usual offenders.
  • Planted early, with the cane. The intercrop goes in within about three weeks of the cane planting, so the cane has its full remaining season to recover from any competition the intercrop caused.
  • Alternate rows, not every row. One intercrop row in every second interrow, not every interrow. Cane bordered by an intercrop on both sides is cane that gets shaded twice.

The reason behind the rules is the cane's own biography. Germination is barely affected by a neighbor, but tiller formation, the shooting of new stalks that decides the season's stand, takes a real dip when the intercrop competes hard. The same research line has a name for what happens when the intercrop leaves on time: the cane recovers, and given water, nutrients, and a clean row afterward, much of the setback disappears by harvest. The cane forgives. The rules exist so it is never asked to forgive too much.

Against those rules, the practical shortlist for a Philippine cane block is short, which is a mercy. Upland rice, the flagship with the research record. Maize, the fastest cash and food crop. Mungbean and the other legumes, the soil builders. Cabbage and the quick vegetables, the high-value option for farms near a market. Each of the next four sections takes one of them apart.

Rice between the rows: the flagship play

Upland rice is the intercrop with the deepest Philippine evidence, and it earns the slot for practical reasons a smallholder feels immediately. The seed is short-duration by breeding, 90 to 95 days for the varieties the DA distributed to the Negros program, drought-tolerant, and planted by dibbling or drilling straight into the interrow with no machinery the farm does not already own. The NRCP trial's parameter range doubles as a starting prescription: rice in furrows 1.0 to 1.5 meters apart matches the cane's own geometry, and seeding rates of 40 to 80 kilograms per hectare both produced, with the denser stand on the tighter spacing taking the top score. The rice's harvest lands at three to four months, exactly as the plant cane canopy arrives, and the palay goes home to the household or straight to the trader.

The economics are the quiet part. Twelve sacks of rice per hectare of cane, the Philsurin figure the Negros program built on, is not a fortune, but it is rice the family was previously buying at the palengke during the exact months cane cash flow dips, and nearly 20,000 pesos per hectare of additional income, the NRCP trial's figure, is a number that changes a small farm's year. The straw is the second dividend: it feeds the compost windrow or the ferments instead of the burn pile, which is the whole argument of our composting guide and the rice straw biofertilizer guide, and it lands in season, when the pile is hungry.

On our own blocks the rice patch between the cane is the household's favorite field, and the reason is timing as much as money. The palay comes off in the middle of the cane cycle, when the farm has been spending on the cane for months and the mill check is still a rumor. A harvest of any size at that moment buys cooperation from every member of the family for the rest of the season. The crop also fits the monsoon calendar the wet season planting guide lays out, because a 90 to 95 day variety dibbled at cane planting rides the rainy months and rarely asks for irrigation the cane blocks do not already get.

Maize and vegetables: the cash crops

Maize is the speed play. The farming community in Flora, Apayao, documented in a LEISA case study by REAP-Canada, intercropped glutinous and sweet maize into their cane for home consumption and fresh market sale, harvested at just 60 days, and planted it only in alternate rows of cane to keep the competition down. That community was also testing white grain maize, pigeon peas, squash, and sweet potato in the same system, which is the honest picture of how this works in practice: a rotating shortlist, adjusted by market and appetite. Maize obeys every rule comfortably, 60 to 90 days, well under two meters for the right varieties, and it has the side benefit the Mauritius researchers noted for intercrops generally: the clean, weeded interrow is weeding the cane's own bed for free.

The vegetables are where the money per square meter gets loud, and where the management gets loud right after it. An extension summary of Philippine cane trials that has circulated for years describes cabbage as a tested intercrop that matures in 60 to 73 days, planted simultaneously with the cane at 0.6 meters between hills and 1.0 meter between rows, with per-hectare gross income estimates running from 306,000 to 503,000 pesos depending on variety. Read that figure the way an accountant would: it is gross, not net, it assumes a complete crop and a buyer at harvest week, and cabbage wants water, feed, and attention that rice between rows never will. What it shows is the ceiling of the vegetable lane, not the floor.

The lane is worth walking for the right farm. A cane block within morning-market distance of a poblacion can turn interrows into pechay, radish, onion, or cabbage money while the cane is still knee high, and the same four rules apply unchanged. The filter we apply before any vegetable goes between the rows is the market, not the seed: name the buyer and the price before the seed goes in the ground, because a vegetable harvest waits for nobody, while a cane harvest has a mill appointment and a rice harvest waits in the panicle. Vegetables suit the farm that sells; rice and maize suit the farm that eats.

Legumes: the quiet soil builders

Mungbean is the classic legume intercrop of Philippine cane country, and it earns its row by doing three jobs with one planting. It fixes nitrogen, which is a modest fertilizer subsidy for the block and a real one for the following season. It produces food and saleable grain at 60 to 70 days on seed cheap enough that a failed stand is an annoyance rather than a loss. And its residues, vines and leaves and roots, go back into the interrow as organic matter, which is the soil program feeding itself. A legume in the interrow is the cover crop module's argument in miniature: living cover through the wet months, terminated by harvest, leaving the ground better clothed than it found it.

Honesty about scale is owed here. An interrow legume is not a full-season cover crop, and the biomass it returns is a fraction of what a dedicated cover stand grows. The regenerative case for it is cumulative, season on season, the same way the compost windrow works: small additions, never skipped, compounding into the soil organic matter trend the measure module tracks. The peanut rows of the previous generation were never measured, but the blocks that got them were the blocks the spade went into easiest, and that observation is older than any of the research above.

The legume lane also handles the risk question better than any other intercrop. If the grain market is soft, the crop still fed the soil. If the season turns dry, mungbean shrugs where cabbage collapses. For a first intercrop on a farm that has never tried one, the sequence we suggest at the gate is mungbean first season, rice second, vegetables only when a buyer is standing next to the field. Order of ambition, learned at the cost of one cheap crop instead of one expensive one.

The rules that keep the cane safe

Feed them separately. The intercrop must live on its own nutrition, never on the cane's. The Mauritius work put numbers on the geometry: with cane rows 1.6 meters apart, maize planted in the middle of the interrow sits 0.8 meters from the cane line, farther than its roots reach, so fertilizer placed in the cane row never feeds the maize and the maize's own feed must be placed in its own line. The rule generalizes: base the intercrop's feeding on what that crop would get as a sole crop, and keep the cane's program exactly as it was. A soil test before the season settles both programs on evidence, which is the soil testing guide's whole case, and it applies to the interrow as much as to the cane row.

Cultivate like a surgeon. Cane culture already prescribes weeding passes around 45 and 90 days, done by digging the ridges and hand-weeding the furrows, with the standing warning not to fill the furrows back in while digging, because buried furrows smother tillering. An intercrop adds a second crop's roots to the same ground and a second reason to be in there with a hoe. Every digging pass in an intercropped block is done slower and shallower than in clean cane, and the intercrop's own rows are weeded by hand. The compensation is real, though: the intercropped interrow suppresses its own weeds, so the field's total weeding labor often drops, which was one of the original appeals of the practice in Mauritius and remains true here.

Water on the cane's rhythm. In irrigated blocks the cane's schedule rules, and the intercrop fits inside it: no new furrow flooding for the intercrop's sake, no water stagnating in the cane furrows because the intercrop wanted a wet week. In rainfed blocks the shortcut is varietal, a 90 to 95 day drought-tolerant rice or a mungbean that rides the monsoon, so the intercrop's water demand is the rain's problem, not the pump's. The water module carries the block-level version of this argument.

Harvest on the clock. The intercrop comes off at physiological maturity, promptly, the day it is ready. Every week it stands past that is a week of shade it now owes the cane, and the recovery the researchers named depends on what happens after removal: water, nutrients, and a clean row. Plan the harvest labor and the drying space the week the intercrop is planted, because a crop that waits on the drying pavement while the cane waits on its light is the most avoidable loss in this whole system.

Respect the ratoon squeeze. A ratoon crop closes canopy at three to three and a half months instead of four to five, so the intercrop window on ratoon blocks is tighter by a month, and the ratoon's own head start makes it the more competitive neighbor. Practically: on plant cane, the full shortlist is available. On first ratoon, favor the 60-day crops, maize and mungbean over rice. On old ratoons, where the stools have encroached into the interrow, the researchers' own suggestion is the paired-row layout, two cane lines close together and a wide alley between pairs, though they report the yield response as variable. The farm's simpler rule: the older the ratoon, the shorter the intercrop.

What the first season pays, and what it costs

Collect the evidence in one place and the honest summary reads like this. Rice between cane: twelve sacks of palay per hectare of cane in the Philsurin study the Negros program stands on, nearly 20,000 pesos per hectare of additional income in the NRCP trial, with the caveat that both are results under competent management. Maize between cane: a 60-day food and cash crop with a generations-long record in Apayao. Cabbage between cane: gross income estimates of 306,000 to 503,000 pesos per hectare in the extension summary, gross being the operative word. Mungbean between cane: food, seed, nitrogen, and residues, at the price of a packet. None of these figures is ours to promise; all of them are attributed, and any farmer who wants the full arithmetic can read the sources named above in an afternoon.

The cost side is the reason smallholders hesitate, so it deserves the same clarity. The seed outlay is small: rice for the interrows is a fraction of a hectare's normal seeding, mungbean cheaper still. No new equipment is needed for dibbled rice, drilled maize, or hand-transplanted vegetables. The real costs are attention and timing: one more crop to feed separately, one more harvest to crew and dry, one more buyer to line up if the intercrop is a vegetable, and the interculture done slower than clean cane. What the intercrop is not is free money. It is a second job taken on the same land, and like any second job it pays only if you show up on time.

What the balance buys, beyond the arithmetic, is the insurance that LEISA's Flora case put into words: with a short crop standing between the rows, a typhoon that flattens the cane no longer wipes out the year's entire income, and the household's food supply stops riding on one mill schedule. On our own blocks the visible cost of the first intercropped season was the tiller dip in the rows that bordered the maize, and the visible gain was the rice patch that carried the kitchen through the lean months. Both matched the research. The cane recovered; the check from the mill arrived; and the intercrop's palay was eaten long before either.

The failures, so you can skip them

The maize that stayed. A long-season maize planted because the seed was at hand, standing fat and tasseling when the canopy wanted to close. The cane's tillering dipped, the light never got home, and the block paid for two seasons. The cure is the 60 to 90 day varieties and a harvest date written on the planting day.

The every-row greed. An intercrop in every single interrow, doubling the stand, shading both of the cane's shoulders. The Mauritius guidance allows one row per interrow but reports the shading as pronounced; alternate interrows is the honest default, and the total harvested area is barely smaller.

The fertilizer theft. The intercrop fed from the cane's band or, just as common, the cane's feed cut because the intercrop seemed to be doing fine on it. Maize roots 0.8 meters from the cane line never touch the cane's fertilizer, which is the geometry that makes separate feeding both possible and mandatory. One feeding program per crop, marked out before the first sack is opened.

The careless digging. The 45 and 90 day weeding passes done at clean-cane speed in an intercropped field, furrows refilled, intercrop roots and cane tillers alike sliced. Tillering loss from a hoe is indistinguishable from tillering loss from competition in the harvest, and it teaches the wrong lesson. Slow down or hand-weed.

The shared pests. Cane and maize share borers, and downy mildew sits over maize across Southeast Asia. The researchers' finding is measured: in the trials reported, borer infestation differences with maize intercropped were negligible, and resistant maize cultivars keep the mildew question closed where it is a problem. They also note that cane grown without insecticides keeps its own biological control agents employed, which is the pests module's position exactly. Choose resistant seed and skip the poison, and the interrow stays an ally.

The ratoon squeeze ignored. A first-ratoon block treated like plant cane, its shorter window stuffed with the same 95-day rice. The rice stood into closing canopy, and both crops paid. Ratoon blocks get the 60-day crops, or nothing, which is a rule that costs one season to learn and one line to obey.

How this fits the method

Intercropping is the method's enterprise stacking in its oldest form: two crops trading the same sunlight on the same land, each leaving the other's job easier. The integrated farming guide stacks enterprises the other direction, livestock and ponds alongside the crops, and the same accounting rules apply to both. The interrow is also the smallest plot the soil module's program runs on: legume residues in, compost out, the organic matter trend in the measure module's notebook, and the making it pay module is where the second income line earns its keep on the ledger. A farm planning its first intercropped season should read the first season module with the interrow in mind, because the same rule holds: the cheapest crop to learn on is the one that fails small.

Frequently asked questions

What crops can you intercrop with sugarcane in the Philippines?

The proven shortlist is upland rice, maize, mungbean and other legumes, and quick vegetables such as cabbage. The NRCP trial used NSIC Rc 27 and PSB Rc 10 rice at 40 to 80 kilograms per hectare, the Negros program distributed 90 to 95 day drought-tolerant rice seed, Apayao farmers run 60-day maize in alternate rows, and extension summaries document cabbage at 60 to 73 days. Whatever the crop, it must obey four rules: short cycle, short stature, planted within about three weeks of the cane, and one row in alternate interrows only.

Does intercropping reduce the sugarcane harvest?

It can dip the cane's tillering while the intercrop stands, but the research verdict is favorable: the NRCP two-year trial found the intercropping system consistently outperformed sugarcane monocropping on land equivalent ratio and its companion measures, and sugarcane remained the primary contributor. Cane recovers from the competition once the intercrop is removed on time, provided water, nutrients, and a clean row. Alternate interrows, prompt harvest, and separate feeding are the practices that keep the cane's own yield whole.

How much extra income does sugarcane intercropping bring?

The attributed figures are: nearly 20,000 pesos per hectare of additional income in the NRCP trial, twenty-three cavans of palay, about twelve sacks of rice, per hectare of cane in the Philsurin study behind Negros Occidental's Sugarcane-Rice Cropping System program, and gross income estimates of 306,000 to 503,000 pesos per hectare for cabbage in a Philippine extension trial summary. The vegetable figure is gross, before costs, and assumes a buyer at harvest week. Actual results depend on crop choice, market access, and management.

When should the intercrop be planted and harvested?

Plant the intercrop within about three weeks of the cane planting, so the cane has most of its season left to recover after the intercrop comes off. Harvest inside the cane's open window: 60 to 95 days for most intercrops, and always before the canopy closes, which happens four to five months after planting on plant cane and earlier, around three to three and a half months, on ratoon. Harvest the day the intercrop is ready, because every late week shades cane that is waiting for its light.

Where to go from here

If you take one thing from this page, take the sequence: mungbean the first season, rice the second, vegetables when a buyer is standing beside the field. The seed for all three costs less than one sack of the complete fertilizer the block was already buying, and the research behind them took three institutions a combined lifetime to assemble.
