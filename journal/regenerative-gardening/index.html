Regenerative gardening: start with what you have

The farm method, scaled down to a bed, a yard, or a handful of pots. Nothing to buy first.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski22 October 2026

Short answerRegenerative gardening is the farm method scaled to a garden: feed the soil, keep it covered, stop disturbing it, grow diversity, and skip the poisons. Start with one bed, compost from kitchen waste, and mulch from what the yard already sheds. The first season buys you the evidence; the second season buys you the harvest.

What regenerative gardening actually means

Regenerative gardening means running a garden the way a regenerative farm runs its fields: the soil is fed rather than stripped, the ground stays covered, disturbance stays minimal, plants are grown in mixes rather than monoculture, and pests are managed by habitat before chemistry. The word is not a product label or a certification for home growers. It is a direction of travel: each season the garden needs less bought input, holds more water, and produces more from the same space than the year before.

The distinction that matters most is against "organic by shopping." A garden can be technically organic and still regressive: bagged organic fertilizer every month, bare soil between plants, a rototiller pass every planting, one crop in one bed. Strip the inputs and nothing is left, because nothing was built. A regenerative garden runs the other way: the inputs are produced on site and each season the system stands more on its own. On our method page we define the same idea at farm scale; the garden version is identical, only smaller and faster to see.

There is also a scale advantage worth naming. A farm transitions over seasons because a mistake across two hectares is expensive. A mistake across two square meters costs an afternoon. The garden is where a farmer, or a future farmer, learns the method's reflexes cheaply: how compost actually behaves, how quickly mulch disappears in the heat, how a bed responds when it is fed instead of tilled. Every lesson costs less in a garden than it does in a field.

The five practices, sized to a garden

The method rests on five practices, and every one of them shrinks to garden scale without losing its point:

  • Keep living roots in the soil. Roots feed the biology underground. In a garden this means never leaving a bed empty after a harvest: pop in a quick cover or a second crop, even if it is just mung bean or a scattering of pechay you will thin for greens.
  • Keep the soil covered. Bare soil in the tropics is soil being destroyed by sun and rain in real time. Mulch it, plant it, or shade it. A bed should almost never show naked dirt.
  • Disturb it as little as possible. Stop digging. Open a planting hole, drop the seed or the seedling, close it. The soil's structure and fungal networks are the garden's real infrastructure, and every double-dig tears a lane through them.
  • Grow diversity. Mixed beds, not soldier rows of one crop. Tomatoes with basil, eggplant with marigold, calamansi understoried with ginger. Diversity confuses pests and feeds a wider soil community.
  • Feed the biology, not the plant. Compost, mulch, and liquid feeds made from farm or garden waste. The plant is fed by the soil life; feed the soil life.

None of these requires a purchase. That is the test of whether you are doing regenerative gardening or just gardening with extra steps: count the things you had to buy. A first season needs seed and nothing else, if the yard supplies the mulch and the kitchen supplies the compost.

Soil first: stop stripping, start feeding

The fastest way to see whether a garden is regenerative is to look at its soil. Stripped soil is pale, powdery when dry, crusted after rain, and empty of worms. Fed soil is dark, crumbles, smells sweet and foresty, and pulls apart into crumbs with roots running through it. The difference is not genetics or luck. It is whether organic matter has been going in faster than it is washing out.

In a tropical garden the stakes are higher than the textbook suggests, because a Laguna downpour can move a centimeter of topsoil in an afternoon if the ground is bare. Everything else on this page protects that thin dark layer, because it is the entire bank account of the garden. Our soil module covers the farm version of this in depth: what soil health actually is, the four cheap tests, and the signs that tell you the biology is coming back. The garden signs are the same, just visible in weeks instead of seasons: worms return first, then the crumb structure, then the color.

Compost from what the kitchen already throws away

A regenerative garden makes its own fertilizer, and the input stream is already in your kitchen. Vegetable trimmings, coffee grounds, eggshells, spent leaves from the garden itself: all of it becomes compost, and compost is what converts dead ground into living soil. The bin does not need to be pretty or expensive. A shaded corner, a pile kept as moist as a wrung sponge, layered with dry leaves or shredded cardboard, turned when you remember to, is a working bin.

Two tropical notes from our own bins, because this is where imported advice misleads. First, heat: a pile in Laguna works year round and finishes in weeks in the wet season, not the months that temperate guides promise, so do not panic if yours seems fast. Second, balance: kitchen waste alone goes slimy and sour. Every bucket of greens wants roughly the same volume of browns, and dry yard waste is usually free for the picking. When the pile smells right, like forest floor, it is right. The full farm-scale process, including the rice-straw and manure versions, is in the compost module.

Cover the soil: mulch is doing more work than any spray

If compost is the garden's bank deposit, mulch is the roof over the vault. A mulched bed stays moist twice as long between waterings, does not crust, grows half the weeds, and does not wash away in the wet season. The mulch itself does not need to be bought. Dry leaves, rice straw, coconut husk chips, sugarcane tops, grass clippings dried a day in the sun: a garden generates mulch as a byproduct of existing.

On our farm the straw used to be burned, because that is what the fields around us did and what we had always done. The year we stopped, the mulch went onto the beds instead of into the air, and the difference showed up that same season: watering dropped, the beds stayed workable in the rain, and the worms arrived. In the garden the same rule applies with whatever the yard sheds. Lay it thick, five centimeters or more, and keep it off the stems of young plants. The cover module covers the farm version, including living covers between rows; in a garden, a living mulch of creeping herbs under taller crops does the same job and looks better doing it.

Dig less: the no-dig bed

The most counterintuitive instruction in regenerative gardening is to stop digging, because digging reads as hard work and hard work reads as virtue. But every spade pass through an established bed tears the fungal threads and crumbs the soil spent a season building, and every rototiller pass does it at industrial speed. A no-dig bed is started once: cardboard or a thick layer of compost laid straight over the existing ground, mulch on top, and planting straight into it. After that it is only ever topped up, never turned.

The results are not subtle. No-dig beds hold together in heavy rain because their structure is intact, they need less water, and their weed seedbank stays buried instead of being hauled up to germinate. Our tilling module makes the full case at farm scale, including what a carabao-drawn plow actually costs the soil over a decade; the garden version of the same argument fits in one sentence: if you must disturb, disturb the least possible area for the shortest possible time.

Pests without poisons at garden scale

A garden is the easiest place in agriculture to skip the poisons, because the scale works in your favor: a pest outbreak you can see across is an outbreak you can end by hand. Squash the beetles, crush the egg clusters on the leaf undersides, hose off the aphids, and be done. The system work sits underneath the hand-picking: diverse beds confuse pests that hunt by smell, flowering herbs and weeds feed the wasps and ladybirds that eat them, and healthy soil grows plants that survive a bite without collapsing.

What you are giving up is the calendar spray, and it is worth naming why the calendar spray loses. It kills the pests and their predators together, so the pest, which rebounds faster, returns first and returns harder, and the garden ends up needing the spray to keep the problems the spray created. If a specific pest is winning, the response in this method is habitat and hand control first, then a targeted, biodegradable spray made from kitchen ingredients as the last resort, aimed at the affected plant rather than the whole garden. The pests module covers the farm versions, including the farm-made sprays we actually mix.

Tropical timing: what imported advice gets wrong

Most regenerative gardening content is written for temperate climates and quiet seasons, and in the Philippines both assumptions are wrong. The wet season is not a pause; it is the main event. Rain leaches nutrients, mulch composts itself into the beds in weeks, fungal disease pressure peaks, and the sun comes out hard enough between fronts to scorch a bed that just lost its cover. The dry season, not winter, is the season that tests water and shade.

On our farm the calendar runs on observation rather than imported months. The calamansi tells you the wet season has arrived properly when its new leaves come in flush; the squash tells you the dry spell is real when its leaves wilt by mid-morning despite moist soil. Beds that carried tomatoes through November want eggplant or greens when the rains settle in, not another tomato. None of this is secret knowledge; it is the habit of writing down what your own square meters did, which is the same notebook habit the farm runs on. A garden teaches it in weeks because the feedback loop is short and the stakes are a bed, not a harvest.

The first-season garden plan

Because the first season should be evidence-gathering rather than heroics, here is the whole plan:

  1. Pick one bed. One to two square meters, visible from your kitchen window. Visibility is not decoration; you will act on what you walk past daily.
  2. Lay it down no-dig. Cardboard or compost straight over the ground, mulch on top. Water it once and let it sit a week.
  3. Start the compost bin the same day. The bin is the garden's engine, and the kitchen produces input regardless of your mood.
  4. Plant a mix, not a row. Something fast (pechay or mustasa), something climbing (sitaw or cucumber on a string), something resilient (kamote as living mulch on the edges), and one flower for the predators.
  5. Write four things down weekly: what you planted, what you picked, what the pests did, what it rained. The notebook is what turns a season of gardening into a season of evidence.
  6. Skip every purchase you can. The point of season one is proving the system produces its own inputs, not assembling a shed full of bottles.

That is the entire plan, and it is deliberately the same shape as the farm's first-season plan: start small, cover the soil, compost what exists, write it down, skip the big purchases. A gardener who runs this season has, without ceremony, run the farm transition's pilot program. The Starter page is the same path written for someone moving on to real growing area.

The honest mistakes, so you can skip them

These are the mistakes we see (and made), listed so the first season's failures can be small ones:

  • Too much garden. Three beds abandoned is less food than one bed tended. The ambitious first garden is the most common failure in this whole space.
  • Compost all greens. The slimy, sour pile that follows a kitchen-only diet convinces more people that composting is gross than any other single mistake. Browns are free; use them.
  • Mulch too thin. Two centimeters of mulch is decoration. Five centimeters is weather protection. When in doubt, add more.
  • Digging by habit. The relapse is real: somewhere in month two, the fork comes out to "fluff" the bed, undoing a season of structure. Fluff is what the worms are for.
  • Spraying at the first hole in a leaf. A leaf with a hole is a leaf that fed something. The garden that needs zero imperfection will demand chemistry forever.

Frequently asked questions

What is regenerative gardening?

Gardening by the five practices of regenerative farming, scaled to beds and yards: feed the soil with compost you make, keep every surface covered with mulch, disturb the soil as little as possible, grow diverse mixes, and manage pests with habitat and hands before bottles. Each season the garden needs fewer bought inputs than the year before.

Is regenerative gardening the same as organic gardening?

No, and the difference shows in the shopping list. Organic gardening swaps synthetic inputs for approved purchased ones. Regenerative gardening works to need fewer inputs of any kind, because compost, mulch, fertility, and pest control are produced on site by the system itself. A garden can be organic and still degenerate; it cannot be regenerative and do so.

How do I start a regenerative garden in the tropics?

Start one small bed no-dig: cardboard or compost laid straight on the ground, five centimeters of mulch on top, planted with a mix of one fast crop, one climber, one living mulch, and one flower. Start the compost bin the same day. Time the work to the rain, not the imported calendar, and write down what the bed does weekly for one season.

Does a regenerative garden save money?

Yes, and the savings arrive before the harvests do. The first season's costs are seed and water, because compost comes from kitchen waste and mulch from yard waste. The fertilizer, mulch, and pesticide lines in the garden budget fall to near zero and stay there, which is the same arithmetic that makes the farm version work, just at a scale where you can watch it happen monthly.
