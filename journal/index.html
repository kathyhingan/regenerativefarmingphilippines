The journal

Field guides from the beds and paddocks, not from the theory.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski28 October 2026

Short answerThe journal is where the method meets particular crops, seasons, and situations. Every guide is written from our own beds and paddocks in Calamba, Laguna, and every guide ends in the same place: which module of the method to read next. New guides land through the week, announced to the waitlist first.

What this journal is, and what it is not

This journal is the applied layer of the method. The ten modules teach the system: what regenerative farming is, how soil is rebuilt, how compost and biofertilizers are made from what the farm already produces, and how the money works. The journal takes those same practices into specific situations, because the questions that actually arrive are never generic. They are specific: what do I do with rice straw besides burn it, when do I plant in a wet season that will not stop, how does this work on a balcony instead of a hectare.

What it is not is a content farm. There is no posting calendar to satisfy here, no article written to fill a keyword slot, and no advice we have not run on our own ground first. Every guide below carries something the textbooks miss, usually because the textbook was written for a different climate and we had to find out which parts survive a Laguna wet season. If a guide cannot honestly say that, it does not get written. That is also why the cadence is monthly rather than weekly: a guide written from a season takes a season to write.

How a guide gets made

Because the journal's whole value rests on its claims being checkable, the process behind each guide is worth stating plainly. Every piece passes through the same five gates before it appears here:

  • A real question starts it. Not a keyword, a question: from a farmer at the barangay meeting, a neighbor at the gate, or a waitlist signup's biggest-cost answer. If nobody has actually asked, the topic waits.
  • The farm tests it. We do not write about a practice until we have run it on our own ground long enough to have gotten it wrong once. The mistakes are part of the guide; a method that never fails on paper has not been tried.
  • The numbers come from our notebook. Costs, yields, and timings in the guides come from the farm's records, in pesos and days, not from memory. Where a figure belongs to someone else, it is attributed to them by name.
  • The association reads it. The barangay Farmers Association includes growers with forty more seasons than us, and the guides that name a local practice get checked with them before publishing. The corrections they hand back are usually the best part of the piece.
  • It stays revisable. Every guide carries its last-updated date, and when the farm learns better, the guide changes. The date is a promise that the piece tracks reality, not a formality.

That process is slower than content farming by design. The cadence follows the farm rather than a calendar: guides land when a season has actually taught something, which lately has meant faster than the monthly pace we first promised. It is also why the guides read the way they do: specific about weather, honest about failures, and short on certainty the farm has not earned. The same rule that governs the story page governs every paragraph here: nothing claimed that we cannot walk you over and show you.

How to use the journal with the method

Read the journal as the case studies and the method as the textbook, and let each send you to the other. The cleanest path for a newcomer is short:

  • Start with the definition, so the word regenerative means one thing to you and not five.
  • Read the guides below that match your situation: a garden, a smallholder plot, a season you are staring down.
  • When a guide names a practice you want to run at scale, follow its link into the module that teaches it properly.
  • Take the Find Your Path quiz when you want the method itself to tell you where to start, based on what you grow, how you farm today, and what your biggest cost is.

The order matters less than the habit: journal for the specific, modules for the system. A farmer who reads only guides collects tricks; a farmer who reads only modules collects theory. The pair is what actually changes a farm.

The guides

Seven guides are live now, and the queue behind them is listed honestly below. New guides are announced to the waitlist first, then linked here.

Regenerative gardening: start with what you have

The farm method scaled down to a bed, a yard, or a handful of pots. It covers the five practices at garden scale, no-dig beds, compost from kitchen waste, mulch that survives a tropical wet season, pests managed by habitat and hand, and a first-season plan that requires nothing but seed. It is the shortest distance between curiosity and evidence, because a mistake across two square meters costs an afternoon, and every reflex a gardener builds there transfers to the paddock.

Regenerative vs sustainable agriculture: the real difference

The two words travel together in plans and on labels, and the merging costs farmers money. This guide separates them by what each one demands of a season: input substitution versus input production, compliance metrics versus trend lines, a flat purchase list versus a shrinking one. It adds the two neighbors the comparison always drags in, organic and biodynamic, and ends with the three questions we ask a farmer at the barangay meeting when they want to know which direction their ground needs.

Integrated farming in the Philippines: one land, many harvests

Crops, livestock, and fish on the same lot, each enterprise's waste becoming the next one's input. This guide walks the full loop map, straw to bedding to compost to bed, manure to pond to fish to muck, ducks to pest patrol, then gives the starter sequence in the order that actually holds: compost first, poultry second, pond last, and only in the low corner where water already collects. It closes with the strains nobody prints in the leaflets: skill, labor peaks, feed, and disease, and where integration stops being regenerative.

Composting farm waste: the pile instead of the burn

The country's cheapest fertilizer is the one most farms set on fire. This guide turns rice straw, manure, and crop waste into the windrow that feeds the beds: the browns and greens already on the lot, the pile method that survives tropical rain, the in-field option for rice land, and the four failures that sour beginners on the whole idea, priced so you can skip them.

Rice straw to biofertilizer: what a small farm needs

The country's biggest waste stream and its cheapest input are the same pile of straw, and the liquid ferments are the fast lane between them. This guide runs the three ferments the farm actually operates: fermented plant juice from weeds and tops, indigenous microorganisms cultured from the farm's own floor, and the bran carrier that points the practice at the paddy, with the equipment list, the government's application schedule, and the four failures priced in sugar.

Reading the wet season: when to plant, when to wait

The Philippine rainy season is not a pause but the main event, and the signs that it is truly settled are visible a week before the forecasts agree. This guide runs the bureau's actual criteria against the four signs the farm reads, lays out the regional planting and harvest windows for rice and vegetables, prices the typhoon strait that the traditional wet season date harvests into, and ends with the lopsided bet that decides the week: waiting costs days, planting early costs seasons.

Soil testing: read the ground before you feed it

The most expensive input is the sack the ground never needed, and the habit that buys it is curable at three price points. This guide walks the free checks the farm reads on foot, the color-chart kit and PhilRice's paddy diagnostic that sort pH, N, P, and K for the cost of two kilos of rice, and the laboratory route with its published price list, its free municipal-letter route, and the mobile labs now parking in the barangays. It closes with the part that pays: what each number actually changes about the season's order of spending.

What is coming, and why these topics

The queue is built from the questions farmers actually ask us, in barangay meetings, at the farm gate, and in the waitlist signup itself. The next guides in the pipeline:

  • Intercropping vegetables in sugarcane: the first season. The classic Laguna pairing, and what the first year of it actually pays.
  • Finding regenerative farms and food near you. How to read a farm's claims, what to ask at the stall, and why the label on the egg matters less than the ground under the hen.

Each of these exists because someone asked. If the question you have is not on the list, the waitlist form has a field for exactly that: the biggest cost or the biggest confusion on your ground. The questions that arrive there set the queue's order.

Who writes this

The journal is written by the family behind Viking Farms PH in Calamba, Laguna: three generations on the same land while the lots around it turned industrial, currently farmed by Willy Hingan (dragonfruit, rice, sugarcane, vegetables, livestock) with Kathy Hingan and Daryl Urbanski teaching the method and the arithmetic around it. The father also leads the barangay Farmers Association, which is where half of these guides get their questions, and where the other half get fact-checked before they are written.

The rule that governs everything here: nothing is claimed that the farm has not measured, and everything imported is tested against our ground before it is repeated. Where a claim belongs to someone else (a research figure, a market number), it is attributed. Where we do not know, the guide says so. A farming family teaching under its own name has one asset, which is that its claims can be checked by walking over; the journal is written so that a walk-over proves it right.

Frequently asked questions

How often do new journal guides come out?

When the seasons hand us a lesson. Each guide is written from actual practice on the farm, which sets the pace: we would rather ship one guide you can act on than four you have already read elsewhere. New guides are announced to the waitlist first and linked on this page the same day.

Can I request a guide for my crop or situation?

Yes, and that is the intended way the queue gets set. The waitlist form asks for your crop, your farm size, and your biggest cost, and those answers already reorder what we write next. If a guide would help many farms with the same question, it moves up; the queue at the bottom of the what-is-coming section above is the honest current order.

Is the journal free to read?

Every guide on this page is free, now and later. The paid product is separate: a ten-module course and a starter kit, both in the works, with the waitlist hearing first when doors open. The journal exists to prove, piece by piece, that the method on this site is worth learning in full.

Start with the guide, or start with the quiz

If you garden or want to, read the regenerative gardening guide first; it is the method at its smallest and cheapest scale. If you farm and want the system, take the Find Your Path quiz and let your own answers pick the module. Either way, joining the waitlist keeps you close to the work: one email when the course opens, the new guides as they land, no obligation in between.
