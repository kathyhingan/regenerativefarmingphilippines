Regenerative vs sustainable agriculture: the real difference

Two words used as if they were one. On our ground they point in different directions, and the difference decides what you do next season.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski23 October 2026

Short answerSustainable agriculture aims to keep a farm from getting worse: hold the soil, hold the yield, hold the inputs steady. Regenerative agriculture aims to make it measurably better: more organic matter, more biology, fewer purchased inputs every season. The difference is the direction of travel, and the shopping list is where it shows first.

The short answer: direction, not degree

Sustainable agriculture asks one question: how do we keep farming this land without running it down. Its target is a steady state. Inputs stay level, soil stays where it is, yields hold, and the farm can go on for generations without visibly damaging the ground, the water, or the neighborhood. It is an honest and necessary goal, and most of agriculture has not even reached it.

Regenerative agriculture asks a different question: how do we leave this land better than we found it, on a schedule we can measure. Its target is a trend. Soil organic matter rises, the soil holds more water, the farm buys less every season, and the biology underfoot visibly returns. Where sustainability holds the line, regeneration moves the line.

The difference matters because the two words demand different decisions. A farm holding steady buys about the same things every season and watches compliance, not trend lines. A farm regenerating expects its purchase list to shrink and its notebook to fill with measurements that improve. If you plan your season by the first logic, the second looks like a hobby. If you plan by the second, the first looks like slow decline with good manners. On our definition page we give the full definition of regenerative agriculture; this guide is about the word people most often confuse it with.

Why the two words get swapped, and where that goes wrong

The swap happens because the two words travel together. Government plans and donor brochures print phrases like sustainable and regenerative agriculture as a single unit, and retailers use both on the same label. After enough years side by side, the words read as synonyms, the way a farmhouse and a house behind a farm would if nobody ever visited either.

The trouble is that merging the words erases the measurable half. Sustainability has been diluted by decades of use: any farm can call its practices sustainable, and there is no universally enforced test behind the word on a food label. Regenerative is now traveling the same road, slapped onto packaging with nothing behind it. When a word carries no test, it stops carrying information, and the farmer who plans by it is planning by decoration.

Our rule, on the site and on the ground, is that a claim must be checkable by walking over. The barangay Farmers Association has growers with forty more seasons than us, and none of them will be impressed by vocabulary. What impresses them is a bed that holds together in a downpour and a fertilizer bill that shrinks. That is the standard we hold both words to: which one can you verify from a carabao track and a receipt book, not from a brochure.

The difference between regenerative agriculture and sustainable agriculture, in practice

Walk the two logics through a real season and the gap stops being philosophical. It shows up in four ordinary places.

Where inputs come from. The sustainable farm substitutes: synthetic fertilizer gives way to approved organic fertilizer, still bought, still delivered, still on the receipt. The regenerative farm produces: compost turned from the farm's own waste, biofertilizer brewed from rice straw, mulch from what the field sheds. Substitution changes what you buy. Production changes whether you buy at all.

What happens to the soil. Sustainable practice aims to keep the soil it has: prevent erosion, maintain organic matter, stop the bleeding. Regenerative practice aims to rebuild soil that is already gone, because most farmland in a long-farmed place like Laguna has already lost half of what it once held. One logic protects a bank balance; the other pays into it.

What gets measured. The sustainable farm measures compliance: limits observed, certifications current, records kept for the auditor. The regenerative farm measures trend lines: does the soil crumble darker this year than last, does the bed need watering less often in the same month, did the compost come entirely from the farm this season. Compliance asks whether you broke a rule. A trend line asks whether the ground is better, and it is the only question that ends with the farm standing on its own.

What the money does. A sustainable farm's input budget sits flat, by design. A regenerative farm's input budget is expected to fall, season over season, as the farm's own biological engines come online. That falling line is what our making it pay module is built around, because on a small Filipino farm the fertilizer line is usually the biggest single cost the method attacks.

The shopping-list test

Here is the test we actually use, and the one we teach first because it needs no laboratory and no paperwork. Walk to the shed and read the list of things the farm must buy every season to keep going. Then read last season's list, and the season before that. If the list is the same length three years running, the farm is sustainable at best. If the list is shrinking while the soil turns darker and holds together in the rain, the farm is regenerating. If the list is growing, neither word applies, whatever the signage says.

Our own list told the story before we ever used the word. The rice straw used to be burned every season, because that is what every surrounding field did and what we had always done. The year the straw went to the compost windrows and the beds instead of into the air, one line of the purchase list started quietly disappearing, and the fields next door kept their smoke. Nothing about that year was high technology. It was a decision about direction: the straw becomes next season's fertility, or it becomes smoke, and only one of those pays the farm.

One honest caveat, because the test cuts both ways. A farm can be genuinely regenerative on its soil and still buy things it cannot yet make: feed for livestock, seed for crops the farm does not save. Regeneration is a direction per line of the list, not a purity badge for the whole farm. Any farmer selling you a permanent endpoint is selling something; the ground itself only ever reports a trend.

Regenerative organic vs organic

Organic certification is an input standard: it defines what may not be used, mostly synthesized chemicals, and what may, with limits. That is a real and useful floor, and we are glad it exists. But notice what the standard does not say: it never requires the soil to improve. It requires the forbidden inputs to stay out.

Which leaves a hole that farmers know and shoppers mostly do not. A farm can be fully certified organic and still be degenerating: bare soil open to the monsoon between plantings, a rototiller pass every cycle, bags of approved organic fertilizer arriving monthly to replace fertility the farm should be making. Strip the deliveries and nothing is left, because nothing was being built. Our regenerative gardening guide makes the same point at garden scale with the same shopping-list logic, and the soil module shows what rebuilding looks like when it is done on purpose.

Regenerative organic, as a phrase, is the industry's attempt to bolt the direction of travel onto the input standard, and as intent we agree with it. Our working version is simpler and we can check it ourselves: treat organic as the floor, the things that must never touch the ground, and regenerative as the slope, the trend line the ground itself must show. The paperwork is the floor's business. The slope is the farm's.

Biodynamic vs regenerative farming

Biodynamic farming is the other word that turns up in these conversations, and it is built the opposite way around. Biodynamic is prescriptive: a fixed set of practices, preparations, and timing rules, with certification issued by an international body that audits the prescriptions. A biodynamic farm is judged by whether it did the prescribed things.

Regenerative farming is outcome-first: it prescribes results, not rituals. Which cover crops, which compost recipe, which tillage schedule, all of it varies by place, and the judgment is made on the ground's trend lines rather than on conformity to a calendar. A regenerative farm may happily borrow a biodynamic practice that works on its soil, and skip the ones that do not, without losing the name.

For a Philippine smallholder this distinction is practical, not academic. Prescriptions written for European estates transfer poorly to a monsoon climate, a carabao economy, and a two-hectare plot; we have watched imported calendars fail on our own ground in the ways the gardening guide describes. Outcomes transfer. The demand that soil improve, that inputs shrink, that water behavior change: those travel anywhere, and they are the actual content of regenerative farming.

Sustainable vs regenerative agriculture: which does your ground need

If the ground is already degraded, and in the places we farm that is the common case, sustainability alone is not enough, and the reason is simple: you cannot sustain a degraded state into health. Holding a stripped, crusted, quiet field exactly as it is preserves the damage at a steady rate. Degraded ground needs the regenerative direction first, and the sustainable habits afterward as the maintenance crew, not the mission.

If the ground is genuinely healthy, keeping it so is the sustainable part, and it is real work. But the regenerative practices still belong in the plan, because every one of them widens the margin: more organic matter means more water held through a dry spell, more biology means more resilience against the pest season, a shrinking input list means more of the harvest money stays on the farm. The two words are not rivals for the same job. One is the floor. One is the slope. A farm needs both, and only one of them gives you a direction to steer by.

When a farmer at the barangay meeting asks us which their farm needs, we answer with three questions instead of a definition. Is any soil visibly bare this week. Is the buy-list the same length as three seasons ago. Does anyone on the farm write down what the ground did this month. Three noes mean the regenerative direction starts today. Three yeses mean the farm is already regenerating and the job is to keep the records honest.

What the difference means on a Philippine farm

Imported sustainability language is written for temperate farms and quiet weather, and the Philippines breaks both assumptions. Here the wet season is not a footnote; it is the main event. A bare bed in a Laguna downpour loses topsoil while you watch, which makes the sustainable goal of holding soil and the regenerative practice of covering it the same conversation held at different urgencies. The difference is that sustainability says prevent erosion as a principle. Regeneration schedules the cover crop, the mulch layer, and the living groundcover against the actual rain, because the rain is the clock the farm answers to.

The money difference is just as local. On a small farm the fertilizer line is the biggest recurring cost the method attacks, and the regenerative answer is to stop buying what the farm can brew: compost from the straw and the manure, liquid biofertilizer from the same stream, mulch from the field's own waste. Our compost module and the soil module carry the full process in pesos and days, because a falling purchase line is the proof that separates the word from the work.

And the words matter here in one more way that imported guides never mention: in a barangay, your claims are checked by people who can see your field from the road. A label impresses nobody at the meeting. A field that holds in the rain, a compost windrow where the burn pile used to be, and a fertilizer receipt book that gets thinner year by year impress everyone. That is the real difference between the two words in the end. Sustainability is a promise about the future. Regeneration is a receipt from the past three seasons, and receipts are the currency farms actually trade in.

Frequently asked questions

Can a farm be both sustainable and regenerative?

Yes, and a mature regenerative farm is the strongest version of both. The regenerative practices move the ground upward each season, and the sustainable habits, the ones that prevent damage, keep the gains from leaking back out. The floor and the slope are not rivals; a farm needs the slope to improve and the floor to keep what it earns.

Is organic farming regenerative?

Not automatically. Organic is an input standard that keeps forbidden chemicals out; it does not require the soil to improve. A certified organic farm can still be degenerating if its soil stays bare, its tillage stays heavy, and its fertility arrives in purchased bags. Organic is the floor, and regenerative is the slope above it.

What is the main difference between sustainable and regenerative agriculture?

The direction of travel. Sustainable agriculture aims to hold the line: the soil, the yield, and the inputs stay roughly level so the farm does no further harm. Regenerative agriculture aims to move the line: soil organic matter, water behavior, and on-farm input production all improve measurably, season over season, and the purchase list is where it shows first.

Which one should a beginner start with?

Start with the regenerative direction, because most Philippine farmland is already degraded and holding a degraded state steady is not recovery. The first moves are small and cheap: cover every bare surface, start one compost bin, stop disturbing the soil more than the planting requires, and write down what the ground does. Sustainability habits then protect what the regenerative work builds.
