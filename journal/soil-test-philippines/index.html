Soil test Philippines: read the ground before you feed it

The most expensive fertilizer on any Philippine farm is the sack that was never needed. This guide is about the habit that buys it, and the read that ends it.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski28 October 2026

Short answerA soil test ends the guessing that buys fertilizer the ground never needed. The walk-over checks are free, the kit sorts pH, N, P, and K into low, medium, and high, the lab gives numbers with a recommendation attached, and every result changes what the farm buys first.

The most expensive sack on the farm

Stand at the input dealer's counter at planting time and watch the transactions. The same sacks go over the counter in the same combinations, season after season, and the reasons behind them are never the ground. One farmer buys what his grandfather bought. One buys what the neighbor bought, on ground that is not his ground. One buys whatever combination the dealer's promo is pushing that week. None of the three can say what their soil already holds, which is why the country's most reliable harvest is the fertilizer industry's.

Our waitlist form asks every farmer for the biggest cost on their ground, and the answer that comes back, again and again, is fertilizer. Not seed, not labor, not land. Fertilizer, priced by the sack, bought on schedule, applied by habit. The habit has a shape you can recognize from a distance: complete fertilizer on everything because complete feels safe, urea pushed hard on rice because everyone knows nitrogen makes green, and the same program on a sandy corner as on the black bottomland, because the sacks were already on the cart.

Feeding a field without reading it is the input-side version of planting into a false start, and the wet season planting guide is where we priced that mistake on the seed side. It feels like action. It pays out like a guess. The guess has three failure modes at once: money spent on nutrients the ground already holds, money not spent on the deficiency that is actually capping the yield, and a soil that drifts more acidic or more depleted every season while the sacks stay identical. The fix is not a product. It is a read, and a Philippine farm has three levels of read available, from free to a few hundred pesos to a laboratory panel.

The walk-over: what the ground says for free

Any soil test Philippines farmers pay for should begin before the payment, on foot, at the surface. The laboratory confirms. The walk decides where to dig, and after a few seasons of practice the walk alone will catch most of what ails a block. We run it at land preparation every season, because the ground reads differently wet and dry, and the measure module carries the notebook where the reads get compared year against year.

  • The smell test. Pull a fistful from spade depth and squeeze it. Ground with a working biology smells sweet and earthy, the same smell that rises off a warm road when rain first hits it, because the same family of soil organisms makes both. A sour, vinegary, or rotten-egg smell is the signature of airless, waterlogged ground, and it is the cheapest drainage diagnosis anyone will ever hand you. No kit measures this. Every experienced farmer trusts it.
  • The color read. Dark, crumbly topsoil is organic matter doing its job. Grey and blue-grey mottles down the profile are water sitting where roots want air, the classic compaction or high water table read. A pale, dusty surface that blows off the shovel is depleted ground, whatever the sacks have been doing to it.
  • The feel test. Moisten a pinch and work it between thumb and forefinger. Gritty grains that will not bind are sand. A slick ribbon that takes a polish is clay. Something in between that feels like flour is silt, and the proportions of the three decide how the ground holds water and feed. The slow version is the jar test, a handful of soil in a jar of water, shaken and left to settle into layers overnight, sand first, silt in the middle, clay on top. Texture from a jar costs one jar.
  • The worm count. Turn a spade-square of topsoil on a humid morning and count. Earthworms are the biology's census, and a spade that comes up with none is a report that no kit will contradict. On our ground the worm count tracks the compost history better than any number we have paid for.
  • The root read. Pull or dig up any plant, even a weed, and look at where its roots went. Roots kinked sideways at one clean depth met a plow pan, the compressed layer the disk or the carabao leaves year after year, and no amount of fertilizer fixes a root that cannot go down. The cure is mechanical, a rip or a deep biological break, and the tilling module carries that argument in full.
  • The water behavior. Puddles that sit long after the rain has stopped, a surface that crusts and cracks between showers, wheelbarrow ruts that hold their shape for days. These are the same signs the planting calendar reads for the monsoon, read here for what they say about the ground itself: how fast it takes water in, and what that means for every sack you were about to spread on it.
  • The tin can test. A can with both ends open, an inch of water poured in, a watch. The minutes that water takes to sink are the infiltration rate, the single number that decides whether any fertilizer you spread stays in the root zone or leaves with the next shower. Fast ground needs its feed in small, frequent doses. Slow ground needs drainage before it needs anything from a sack.

One more free read runs in season rather than before it. Rice farmers can track the crop's nitrogen standing by leaf color, using the leaf color chart that agricultural extension workers push at every rice training: the newest fully opened leaf goes against the chart's color panels, and the panel it matches says whether the crop is feeding or starving. It is the only soil-adjacent test the farm runs weekly through a crop, and it costs the chart.

What the walk-over changes is the order of attention, not the order of the sacks. It names the corner that gets the first compost, the paddy that needs drainage before it needs feed, the block whose tiredness is a plow pan rather than a nutrient. Its limit is just as important: the walk names problems, it does not produce numbers. The moment a decision needs a number, lime rates especially, the walk hands off to the kit or the lab.

The kit: low, medium, high

The Department of Agriculture's Bureau of Soils and Water Management makes the national kit, the Soil Test Kit, and its rapid variant for field use, the Rapid Soil Test. The brochures describe the design plainly: reagents and color charts and pictures that tell a farmer whether the soil is acidic or alkaline, and whether nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are low, medium, or high. The kit is built for field use by farmers and agricultural extension workers, no laboratory and no training course required, and the bureau's Laboratory Services Division produces and distributes the kits, the rapid kits, and the refill packs.

The kits are cheap enough that cost is not the argument. On one regional soils laboratory's published service list, a full qualitative kit readout, pH, N, P, and K together, is priced at 130 pesos, and a refill pack at 300. The same laboratory's description of the kit is the endorsement that matters: the qualitative result, it says, is comparable to the quantitative laboratory analysis and can be used in computing a fertilizer recommendation. Low, medium, and high are coarse categories, but they are honest categories, and they are enough to break the blanket habit.

The kit idea has decades of history in this country's universities. The University of the Philippines Los Banos describes its own kit, developed by a succession of soil scientists to make analysis affordable and accessible, as chemical solutions in small bottles that read pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and salinity in a few minutes of color reaction, a decision aid designed for field technicians and users without technical background. Generations of extension workers learned soil testing on exactly this kind of kit, which is why the color chart culture survives in every municipal agriculture office.

The modern piece that closes the loop is software. In May 2024 the bureau launched its Fertilizer Recommendation Mobile Application, known as FertRight, now distributed as a free Android app. You input the sample details, the crop, and the pH, N, P, and K results, and the app generates a crop and site-specific fertilizer recommendation in under a minute, highlighting the three Rs, the right amount, the right kind, and the right timing, with the report exportable as a PDF. This is what turned the kit from a triage tool into a prescription tool: the read is qualitative, but the recommendation computed from it is specific enough to buy against.

For paddies there is a second kit, and it is our favorite piece of low-cost technology in this whole space. PhilRice sells the Minus-One Element Technique kit, MOET, at 300 pesos, and it diagnoses a paddy's soil for deficiency of six nutrient elements with no chemistry at all. The technique stands on the law of the minimum, the old agronomy rule that growth is dictated by the scarcest resource rather than the total, as explained in the Manila Bulletin's writeup of the government's own soil diagnosis recommendations: seven formulations are prepared, one complete and one missing each of the six nutrients, plants are grown in the farm's own soil under each formulation, and whichever minus-one pot underperforms the complete one names the deficiency. It needs a good chunk of soil, around 35 kilograms per field portion being tested, and a week or two of growing, but it answers the question every rice farmer actually has, which nutrient is the bottleneck, for the price of two kilos of rice.

What the kit level changes is the smallest, most common decision in Philippine farming, the sack combination at the counter. A 300 peso MOET that shows potassium high and nitrogen deficient, or a 130 peso kit read that shows pH fine and phosphorus low, is the difference between buying the same complete fertilizer out of habit and buying the specific feed the ground asked for. Over a season the kit can pay for itself in one un-bought sack.

The lab: numbers, and a recommendation attached

The laboratory level is where the categories become numbers: pH to the decimal, organic matter as a percent, phosphorus and potassium in parts per million, and the option of the micronutrients, the cation exchange capacity, and the heavy metals where the history of the land asks for them. Numbers do two things categories cannot. They let you price a correction precisely instead of guessing at it, and they give you a baseline, so next year's sample is a trend line rather than a fresh guess. The measure module is where that line lives.

Everything about a lab result is decided at sampling, which is why the sampling protocol is worth following like a recipe. The farm is divided into partitions that are genuinely uniform, the same cropping history, the same fertilizer treatment, the same soil texture, and the same degree of erosion, with rolling land split into top, slope, and bottom sections. Within each partition, the government's own guidance, as carried in the Manila Bulletin explainer of the national soil diagnosis recommendations, is to dig ten random pits, take the sample from the top 20 centimeters where the nutrients concentrate, and keep the holes away from spots where organic material concentrates, manure piles and straw stacks especially. The ten cores are mixed into one composite per partition. Regional laboratories ask for a minimum of one kilogram of soil per sample, and the sample should be dried before it travels, because a wet, sealed bag keeps changing chemistry on the way to the bench.

The price of all this is published, not rumored. The Regional Soils Laboratory of the Department of Agriculture's Central Luzon field office lists its fees openly: pH at 100 pesos, available phosphorus at 250, exchangeable potassium at 160, organic matter at 250, total nitrogen at 250, cation exchange capacity at 300, and each micronutrient, zinc, copper, iron, manganese, at 160. Texture runs from 30 pesos for the feel method to 300 for the instrumented particle size analysis, and heavy metal screens are 500 each for the farms that need them. A basic fertility panel of pH, available phosphorus, exchangeable potassium, and organic matter therefore lists at 760 pesos per partition, before any specials. That is the number to hold against a season of blind sack purchases.

Now the part most farms never learn: there are routes where that 760 pesos is not charged at all. The first is the regional soils laboratory itself, working through the municipal agriculture office. In our own region, Calabarzon, the laboratory's agriculturist explained the arrangement in an interview carried by the Philippine News Agency's syndication: farmers who bring a letter of request signed by their municipal agriculturist are served free, and students and academics doing research get half off. Every region has its own soils laboratory; the one that serves this farm sits in Lipa City, an hour up the highway from Calamba. The letter costs a visit to the municipal agriculturist, which is also where the staff will show you the sampling protocol they want followed, and the same laboratories test fertilizers and plant tissue when the question is whether a product's label is honest.

The second free route is the truck. The Bureau of Soils and Water Management has been deploying Mobile Soil Testing Laboratories, instrumented ten-wheelers capable of analyzing up to 44 parameters across soil chemical, physical, microbiological, and water properties, and the DA newsroom describes the mobile service as free to farmers growing rice, corn, high-value crops, vegetables, and root crops. All 17 regional field offices are now equipped with a unit. The Tarlac pilot gave the program its proof: 543 soil samples analyzed, 346 fertilizer recommendations issued across 25 high-value crops, 584 farmers and local stakeholders trained in sampling and in the FertRight app. The rollout is still moving: this season, farmers in Bislig City were offered free testing at their City Agriculture Office from September 29 to October 2 under the National Soil Health Program, with farmers asked simply to bring soil samples from their farms. When the truck parks in your municipality, the price argument is over for that day, and the right response is to bring a composite from every partition on the farm.

Submission itself is unglamorous: the request form from the laboratory, the dried and labeled composite, the crop you want the recommendation for. The report that comes back is not a list of numbers looking for interpretation. The regional laboratories are mandated to analyze soil fertility status as the basis for a fertilizer recommendation, the right kind and the right amount, so the report names the crop's band and the amendment, and the farmer's job is to act on it. Ask for the turnaround when you submit, and plan the purchase calendar around it, because the report is only worth a sack's money if it arrives before the sacks are bought.

Reading the report: what each number changes

A report nobody acts on is 760 pesos of shelf art. Here is the read, in the order the farm should act on it, and this ordering is the whole reason we test before we buy anything.

pH first. Acidity is the number that decides whether everything else in the sack can work, because phosphorus in particular locks up in acid ground and most vegetables sulk in it long before rice complains. The report names the crop's comfort band and the correction. Lime is a bulk purchase, a spread, and a schedule, and the mistake farms make is buying it on a neighbor's say-so. Our rule is absolute: no lime sack is bought on any evidence weaker than a laboratory pH. On ground only slightly off the band, the organic matter program is the gentler correction, because a living, well-composted soil buffers its own chemistry over time, and the soil module carries that story.

Organic matter second. The percentage the report prints is the same percentage the compost pile and the cover crop are hired to raise, which makes it the scorecard of the whole method. One report is a snapshot. The same partition, sampled the same season every year, is a trend, and a rising organic matter trend is the receipt the regenerative approach prints for anyone who doubts it. The fixes are already on this site: the composting guide for the pile, the cover crop module for the living phase of it, and the integrated farming guide for the manure streams that feed both.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium third. This is where the report's recommendation does its arithmetic: the right kind, the right amount, the right timing. Where the lab says potassium is already high, the complete-fertilizer line item shrinks to what the ground lacks. Where nitrogen is the deficit, the bought sack sits on top of the cheap first layer, the plant and weed ferments from the rice straw biofertilizer guide, and the two together cost less than the sack alone ever did. The FertRight app, fed the same numbers, produces the same style of recommendation for free and exports it as a PDF for the notebook.

Texture and cation exchange capacity last. These are the numbers that explain history rather than command purchases. Sandy ground with a low exchange capacity cannot be fed once a season; it has to be fed small and often or the feed leaves with the water, and no sack schedule changes that, only organic matter does. This is the number that explains why the neighbor's recipe, transplanted onto your ground, failed. Grounds differ at the level the sacks never reach.

Run the read in that order and the season's spending reorganizes itself: lime before fertilizer where the pH demands it, compost before more nitrogen where organic matter is the real ceiling, and sometimes nothing at all, because the ground is already holding enough. That last outcome is the one the input economy never advertises, and it is the most profitable line item on any farm's ledger.

What skipping it costs

Blind feeding does not fail loudly. It fails as a slow tax: money out for nutrients already present, the true deficit never addressed, and the yield plateau blamed on the variety, the weather, or the farmer's luck. The sacks stay identical, so the ground's response stays identical, and the farmer concludes that farming is hard rather than that the program was never aimed at the ground. A 760 peso panel, or a 300 peso MOET, or a 130 peso kit read is the cheapest audit that farm will ever buy.

Our own rule, after enough wasted sacks in the early seasons, is written into the buying calendar. Walk-over every season, before anything. Kit or MOET before any first big application on ground that is new to us. Full panel before any lime purchase and before any change in a block's crop, because a change of crop is a change of question and the old numbers answer the old question. The making it pay module is where the input bill that these reads trim gets priced properly, and the first season module makes the soil test the first purchase of any farm starting from zero, before the seed, before the sacks.

There is also the argument a test wins socially, which matters more than beginners expect. The regenerative claim is easy to wave away at a barangay meeting until the organic matter trend from the same partition, three years running, is on the table. Re-testing turns the method from an opinion into a receipt, and nothing moves a skeptical neighbor faster than a number that went up while their program stayed flat.

The failures, so you can skip them

Four ways a soil test wastes its own money, all purchased by this farm or its neighbors at full price.

The composite that lies. One convenient scoop from the gate corner, where the manure pile sat for two years, sent in as the field. The laboratory reports faithfully on the pile's shadow, the field gets the correction the pile's shadow asked for, and the real deficiency survives another season. The cure is the sampling protocol taken literally: partitions by history, ten pits, the top 20 centimeters, away from the organics, mixed honestly. A soil sample is a lie of convenience the moment you dig it where it is easy.

The wet sample. Dug after a rain, sealed wet in plastic, left in the sun on the jeep's roof rack. The chemistry keeps working in the bag, the nitrogen readings shift, the lab receives something the farm never actually had. The Calabarzon laboratory asks for samples dried before they are brought in, and the reason is exactly this. Dry in the shade, paper bag, label inside and out.

The kit promoted to a laboratory report. Low, medium, and high cannot give you a rate, and a farmer who budgets lime by the kit's color panel is guessing with extra steps. The kit is triage: it sorts the farm's problems cheaply and tells you which blocks deserve the laboratory's numbers. Rates, trends, and lime decisions come from the lab, or at minimum from the computed recommendation the FertRight app generates from the kit's numbers.

The report in the drawer. Tested once, filed once, and the next season starts from zero, so the farm pays for the same snapshot every few years and never owns a trend. The cure costs nothing but habit: the same partition, the same season, the number copied into the measure module's notebook, the line drawn against last year's. One report is data. A row of them is management, and the difference is the whole return on the 760 pesos.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a soil test cost in the Philippines?

The Regional Soils Laboratory of DA Central Luzon publishes its fees: pH at 100 pesos, available phosphorus at 250, exchangeable potassium at 160, organic matter at 250, and total nitrogen at 250, so a basic panel of pH, phosphorus, potassium, and organic matter lists at 760 pesos. A full qualitative Soil Test Kit readout is listed at 130 pesos with refills at 300, and PhilRice's MOET kit for paddy soil is 300 pesos. Free routes exist through the regional laboratories and the mobile soil laboratories.

Where can I get my soil tested for free?

Two routes are free. The DA regional soils laboratories serve farmers free when they bring a letter of request signed by their municipal agriculturist, an arrangement the DA Calabarzon laboratory's agriculturist described in a Philippine News Agency carried interview, and students get half off. The DA-BSWM Mobile Soil Testing Laboratories, which analyze up to 44 parameters and are now deployed across all 17 regional field offices, test samples free for farmers growing rice, corn, high-value crops, vegetables, and root crops under the National Soil Health Program.

Is a soil test kit accurate enough to farm by?

The kit gives qualitative results, pH and NPK as low, medium, or high, and the DA's regional soils laboratory describes those results as comparable to its quantitative laboratory analysis and usable for computing a fertilizer recommendation. Paired with the free BSWM FertRight app, which turns kit numbers into a right kind, right amount, right timing recommendation, it is enough to break the blanket fertilizer habit. Rates, trends, and lime decisions still deserve the laboratory's numbers.

When should I take a soil sample?

Sample before the season's land preparation, so the report arrives before the fertilizer is bought, and sample the same partition in the same season every year so the numbers form a trend. Avoid sampling right after manure, compost, or lime applications, and avoid freshly rained-on ground. Keep the holes away from manure and straw piles, take the top 20 centimeters, mix ten cores per uniform partition, and dry the sample before it travels, as the Calabarzon regional laboratory asks.

Where to go from here

If you take one thing from this page, take the order of operations: read before you feed. The walk costs a morning, the kit costs 130 to 300 pesos, the panel costs 760 or less than nothing when the municipal letter or the mobile laboratory covers it, and every one of them changes what the sacks say at the counter.
