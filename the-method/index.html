The Method

Ten modules. One farm. Your land next.

By Kathy Hingan & Daryl Urbanski2 October 2026

Short answerThe Method is a ten-module self-paced course teaching regenerative farming as it is practised on a family farm in Calamba, Laguna. It is built for tropical wet and dry seasons, uses inputs the farm already produces, and takes a farmer from a first soil walk to a harvest that pays.

What the course is

The Method is the complete regenerative system this family runs on its own land, written down as ten modules a farmer can work through at their own pace. It is not a philosophy book and not a collection of tips. Each module ends with something to do on your own land this season, and the modules build on each other in the order the transition actually happens.

It ships as short lessons, five to fifteen minutes each, in text and video. Ten focused modules rather than twenty-four sprawling ones, because the point is that a farmer finishes and acts, not that the course looks impressive.

Why it is built for the Philippines first

Almost every regenerative farming book and course on the market teaches a temperate farm. They assume spring planting, fall harvest, a winter that resets the soil, and a growing calendar built for four seasons. The Philippines has two seasons, wet and dry, typhoons instead of snow, and rice paddies that behave like nothing in those books.

That mismatch is where imported advice fails, and it is the reason this course exists. The module on water and seasons is not a chapter about irrigation in general. It is about reading the wet season, timing the dry one, and keeping soil covered when a typhoon is coming. The module on compost is about rice straw, animal manure, kakawate and madre de cacao, and coconut coir, because those are what a farm here actually has.

Every module in this course was farmed before it was written. If it did not work in Laguna, it did not make the course.

The ten modules

In order, and why each one comes where it does:

  1. Why Regenerative, Why Now. Rising input costs, degraded soil, and what the family legacy means. The honest case for changing, before any technique.
  2. Know Your Soil. Living soil science in plain language, and simple tests a farmer can run with their hands, a jar, and a shovel. Worms and roots as health indicators.
  3. Compost and Biofertilizers from What the Farm Already Produces. Rice straw, manure, kakawate, coconut coir. Brewing, applying, and the costs avoided. This is the module that pays for the course fastest.
  4. Stop Tilling. Minimal disturbance adapted to rice paddies and vegetable beds, and what happens to soil structure and weeds when you stop.
  5. Cover the Soil. The seven-layer planting: canopy trees, vines, cash crops, cover crops, roots, groundcover, and animals in the same system. Rotation and intercropping with tropical species.
  6. Pests Without Poisons. Natural pest management, beneficial insects, and homemade sprays from farm materials.
  7. Water and Seasons. The tropical wet and dry calendar, typhoon resilience, and irrigation timing that matches how rain actually arrives here.
  8. The First-Season Conversion Plan. A step-by-step first season on your own land, with the starter kit as its companion chapter.
  9. Making It Pay. Lower input costs, premium sales, direct and cooperative marketing, and dragonfruit as the cash-crop case: the vine layer that pays while the other layers build soil.
  10. Measure and Keep Going. Soil health indicators year over year, when results actually show, and what to do when they do not.

What you will have done by the end

A course that ends with a certificate is not useful to a farmer. This one ends with things done on your own land:

  • A soil walk of your whole farm, written down: where water stands, where the soil is bare, where it is compacted, and what already grows there.
  • One compost pile or brewing setup running on farm materials, with a recipe you can repeat.
  • At least one field or bed with living cover on it through the next season.
  • A season calendar for your own plot, marked with what to plant, when to cut, and when to wait.
  • Cost numbers for your farm before and after, so the change is measured in pesos and not in feeling.

That is the difference between learning about regenerative farming and running it. The modules are the route; those five items are the destination.

What the course is not

Three honest limits, up front:

  • It is not a certification. No certifier owns the word regenerative yet. This teaches the practice; if certification arrives, the farm that does the work will be ready for it.
  • It is not a promise of yield. Soil changes take seasons, and some of the gains show up in cost and resilience before they show up in volume. Anyone promising a harvest number is guessing.
  • It is not a supply of inputs. The whole point is needing fewer bought inputs. The course teaches you to build fertility from what the farm already produces, which is why it costs less to run, not more.

The farm behind the course

The method is taught from Viking Farms PH in Calamba, Laguna, where the same family has farmed for three generations. Rice, sugarcane, vegetables, and calamansi were the original crops. Today the farm's main crop is dragonfruit, with farm animals in the system, and the seven-layer planting described in module five laid out across the land.

The grandfather and grandmother farmed it, the father farms it now, and he leads the barangay Farmers Association, working with the City of Calamba to keep farming alive and farmers educated. The land around the farm converted to industrial use over the decades; the family kept farming. That is the whole proof asset this brand has, and it is enough, because it is real.

Who it is for

If you have decided and need the method, the whole course in order is your path. If you are already regenerative and want to systematize and measure, go straight to nine and ten.

How the modules were chosen

The syllabus was compiled by reading what actually sells and what actually gets finished. The highest-rated regenerative farming books are story-driven rather than technical: a family's conversion outranks the textbooks, which is why module eight is a first-season plan and not a chapter of theory. The single most-reviewed practical topic was making amendments from materials a farm already has, which is why module three is the longest in the course and the one most farmers say paid for the rest.

Everything else was adapted to this climate. Where the source books assume a temperate calendar, the modules substitute the wet and dry seasons. Where they assume cheap land and machinery, the modules assume a smallholder's budget and hand tools.

Frequently asked questions

How long does the course take?

It is self-paced, with lessons of five to fifteen minutes each. Most farmers work through a module a week alongside the work the module asks for, so a full first pass takes about ten weeks, and the season calendar inside it then runs on the farm's own cycle.

Is it for Filipino farmers only?

No, but it is tropical-first. The method is taught from a Philippine farm and timed to wet and dry seasons, which makes it directly useful across Southeast Asia and other tropical regions. Farmers in temperate climates can use the principles, but the calendar sections will need translating to their own seasons.

Do I need the starter kit to start the course?

No. The kit is a companion, not a requirement, and the course is built around inputs the farm already produces. The kit exists for farmers who want the tools, seeds, and setup in one box instead of sourcing them one at a time.

What if my farm is very small, or a garden?

The method works at garden scale and often proves itself faster there. The same five practices apply to a backyard plot, and the compost and cover-crop modules adapt directly. The audience pages explain the path for each farm size.
